For years, I was a Microsoft hater. Windows was weird, Office was way too expensive and clumsy, and then, of course, there was the Windows Phone disaster. For some reason I was convinced Microsoft was a failure, doomed sooner or later. No company can get away with such consistently bad products in the long run.

Then I got an office job for the first time. It blew my mind. We were deep in the Microsoft ecosystem. Azure, Office, GitHub, VS Code, LinkedIn, Teams, Exchange email. Anything and everything Microsoft. And I realised how finely tuned all of it is for employers and big corporations. A walled garden taller than anything Apple can dream of. **I learned that the machinery of large companies is oiled with Microsoft's products.**

## Get them at home, win them at work

It's surprisingly easy to get Windows for free these days. And honestly, Microsoft has never really cracked down on pirated Windows, because it wants people using it at home. That way, when they get to the office, it's the default choice. Everyone is already familiar with it.

It's the same reason so much software comes with free student licences. When a company asks its engineers what software to use, they'll pick whatever they learned at school.

Don't get me wrong. I still think Teams is a bloated product, and the messaging part is genuinely bad. But Teams works seamlessly with everything else Microsoft makes, and if you ignore the chat, it's actually a pretty neat piece of software. LinkedIn, whatever you think of the feed, is the default place where job openings and candidates meet, and it has become one of the biggest social networks by revenue.

Xbox, on the other hand, has been a real disappointment for the last few years. And don't get me started on the Activision acquisition.

## The Death Star

Microsoft is underrated. It came to dominate cybersecurity by bundling the Defender suite into its licences. It won the opening round of the AI race with its exclusive partnership with OpenAI. It utterly commands the office suite market. It's clawing more cloud market share by the day, as one of the two leaders in the space. It overtook Slack, which Salesforce ended up buying, by making Teams free with its licences. And if it gets gaming in order, it's well positioned there too: Activision Blizzard has been badly mismanaged for years, while owning arguably the most valuable set of franchises in the industry.

**This company is the Death Star.** And every year it keeps gobbling up new spaces and expanding its business.

## Looking back from 2026

The OpenAI part of this has changed the most. In 2024 I called it an exclusive partnership. Since then the relationship has loosened: in the [restructuring agreed in October 2025](https://openai.com/index/next-chapter-of-microsoft-openai-partnership/), Microsoft ended up with about 27% of OpenAI and kept exclusive rights to its models in Azure's API, but gave up its right of first refusal as OpenAI's cloud provider. OpenAI now buys compute from several clouds. Microsoft still has a huge stake in the outcome, but it's no longer the only door in.
