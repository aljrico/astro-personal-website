Back in 2018, Tesla was a small carmaker by volume, and yet it was already worth more than Ford or GM. By 2020 it was worth more than several of the biggest carmakers combined. That gap was the main thesis behind the many, many shorts on Tesla.

The bulls' counterargument was simple: "The market isn't pricing Tesla for what it is, but for what it will become." And that future looked bright, as long as a couple of assumptions held:

- The future of transportation is electric and self-driving cars.
- Tesla is far ahead in both.

If both were true, the argument went, Tesla would follow Apple's success story:

- Tesla swamps the market, reaching Apple-like market share.
- That dominance makes aggressive vertical integration profitable.
- Vertical integration widens margins and makes the product better.
- Tesla uses its lead to expand the market itself.

How was it supposed to expand the market? By going where no carmaker had gone. Neither Ford nor anyone else ever had the capacity, appetite or money to own a nationwide network of petrol stations. Tesla claimed it could do the equivalent with proprietary chargers, using its market share. The higher the share, the more profitable the network, and the harder it would be for buyers to choose a competitor locked out of it. Remember, lack of convenient, quick charging is still the number one reason many people buy combustion cars instead of EVs.

If all that worked, there were even more markets to open up. Software, for example. Self-driving was supposed to be an inevitability just a couple of years away, and if Tesla was the only player in town, that was another huge market it had just created. With the same kind of virtuous cycles: more Tesla owners means more data to train the AI, better AI means a better car, and that data could be sold to insurers, who would kill for a piece of it, meaning cheaper premiums for Tesla owners.

That's the story Tesla investors have told each other to justify why a small carmaker should be worth more than its biggest rivals combined. But consider for a moment that this whole house of cards rests on two assumptions. If they fail, the whole thing comes crashing down:

- Electric and self-driving cars are the *immediate* future of transportation.
- Tesla is so far ahead in both that it reaches 80%+ market share and kicks off those virtuous cycles.

I'll grant that EVs are the immediate future of many forms of transport, most importantly personal cars. But that's about all I can give the bulls at this point. It's 2025, almost seven years since the hype train left the station. A mix of market shifts, regulation and the company's own mistakes has stopped the rest of the thesis from materialising. And at this point, I don't think it ever will.

## What happened?

**Tesla is not the only player in town anymore.** Let's be real. You can argue a Model 3 is more ergonomic than a Polestar 2 (which has Volvo behind it), or that its interior is nicer than a Hyundai Ioniq 5's. But those differences don't add up to 80% market share. Buyers have real choices outside Tesla. You can argue the product is better, but not that it's so much better there's no competition. There is competition.

**Regulation has made a charging monopoly essentially impossible.** Even setting aside the market share it would take, which Tesla doesn't have, regulators in Europe, China and the US have pushed for open charging that a single manufacturer can't lock competitors out of. Tesla itself ended up opening its Superchargers to other brands.

**Self-driving cars aren't here yet.** Maybe Tesla isn't living up to the hype. Maybe the technology just isn't ready. Either way, self-driving has been "one year away" for a decade. Maybe this year is the year, but I don't think it's sound judgement to assume so when making investment decisions.

**And most of all, Elon Musk has happened.** Whatever you think of his politics, we can all agree he's an extremely volatile and extremely controversial figure. That always means bad business. I know he has been the driving force behind a lot of what Tesla has done, but at this point I genuinely believe he's doing the company more harm than good. His volatility makes him swing from ignoring the company completely to hyper-focusing on very niche products he personally likes.

To name the elephant in the room: the Cybertruck. However much I may like it, it's an extremely niche product. Nobody can say it's for everyone. That's a bad business decision for a company whose mission is to win huge market share with a handful of products and end-to-end vertical integration. It goes against the company's habits, manufacturing processes and value proposition. It pulls resources away from vertical integration (sprawling into niche products is horizontal expansion, not vertical), its polarising design dilutes Tesla's brand, and the shift in focus let the whole competition catch up instead of Tesla doubling down on its first-mover advantage. Excuse me for being blunt, but it was a stupid decision the company is paying dearly for. In my honest opinion, it was a PR stunt that got out of hand and should never have materialised. Tesla should have used its head start to double down on vertical integration. Either widen margins and pour the cash into R&D, or cut costs and price out the competition. Instead, the company got distracted by unimportant nonsense.

Without getting into his personal views, being this controversial is bad business. Can you name, without Googling, the CEOs of Toyota, Ford, Volkswagen or Porsche? Let's be honest, nobody on the street cares about their latest posts or which party they're lobbying for. And for good reason. Musk has created a situation where owning a Tesla can be read as a political statement. Not everyone is comfortable being put in that position. Which is why Tesla's sales in Europe are now falling by more than 40% while the electric car market keeps growing.

I used to be a Tesla investor. It was my first ten-bagger. I sold long ago, when I saw Elon becoming erratic, pumping and dumping Bitcoin and, simply put, not being focused on being Tesla's CEO. I don't presume to know what his agenda is, but I'm pretty sure it isn't increasing value for Tesla shareholders. The moment I realised that, I got out.
