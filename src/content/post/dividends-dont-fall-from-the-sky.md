Every other day I end up in a discussion about whether dividend investing is the safest option. "It's more reliable," they say. "It's the stock actually paying you back," they claim. "Stocks without dividends are speculation," some even argue.

How true is that, though? I think most people lose sight of where those dividends come from.

They come from companies. You know, money-generating machines. Organisations that use money to make even more money. Sometimes they reinvest that extra money to grow further. Sometimes they hand it to investors, either as dividends or as buybacks.

Dividends don't come from thin air. A dividend is literally cash taken out of the company. It's money the company can't reinvest in the business. It's money that can't go toward paying down debt. It's an opportunity cost on every other investment. The book value of the company goes down when it pays a dividend.

If a company doesn't have enough free cash to pay dividends, it won't pay them. Simple as that. Or it will borrow to keep paying them, which puts its future at risk. That would be silly, right? Guess what: some companies do exactly that so their investors don't freak out when cash flow gets tight.

If something looks too good to be true, it probably is. Look up the company in the S&P 500 with the highest dividend yield, and I promise you'll find a company in deep trouble, not the smartest investment around. It's way too tempting to see a 25% dividend yield and think you'll collect that every year. Chances are the dividend won't last, or the company is simply giving up and slowly liquidating itself.

**There are no dividends without free cash flow.** To decide whether a company is worth investing in, you need to estimate its ability to produce earnings. Follow the money, always. Is the dividend coming from stable free cash flow? Then we're good. But at the end of the day, what matters is the company's ability to produce profit, now and in the future.

And here's the magic trick. Once you fully understand this, you realise you didn't really need the dividend at all. A company that keeps growing its income will grow in value with or without a dividend, and that growth benefits the investor just the same. Even better, in most jurisdictions holding a stock while it grows, instead of cashing dividends, means paying less tax along the way.

Dividends are alluring, and they have their place in certain strategies. But they can be extremely misleading, and they can trick investors into a false sense of security. Failing companies often use them for exactly that, to slow the fall of their stock price. A mummer's farce.
