Risk management sounds boring. It's like being reminded to eat vegetables, exercise or drink water. Everyone agrees it's important, but it's not sexy, so nobody really does it.

I think that's a huge mistake. **Good risk management makes you a richer person, not just a safer one.** Here's why.

## The gain-loss disparity

How much do you need to gain to recover from a loss? Say your portfolio is down 10% today. If it gains 10% tomorrow, are you back where you started? It feels intuitive, but no. You'd still be down 1%.

This is what I call the gain-loss disparity, and it's one of the most confusing ideas in investing. To recover from a 10% loss you need an 11% gain. It's so unfair. And it gets worse the bigger the loss. If you lose a fraction $$L$$ of your money, the gain $$G$$ you need to get back to even is:

$$
G = \frac{1}{1 - L} - 1
$$

Which looks like this:

| Loss | Gain needed to recover |
| --- | --- |
| -10% | +11% |
| -20% | +25% |
| -30% | +43% |
| -40% | +67% |
| -50% | +100% |
| -90% | +900% |

It's mind-blowing how fast the disparity grows as losses get deeper. And it matters enormously when you're managing the risk of a portfolio.

## What real risk looks like

Consider a common example. Plenty of investors will take enormous risks to make +90% in a month, only to lose 90% the next. At first that seems reasonable: risky, but they might get rich. But +90% followed by -90% is a total loss of 81%. Even if they "only" lost 60% the second month, they'd be down 24% overall.

It's easy to get blinded by a big green number and ignore the risk behind it. If your portfolio is down 90%, you need a 900% gain to get back. What are the chances of doing that in a reasonable amount of time? At a market-average return of 7% a year, it would take you about 34 years ($$1.07^{34} \approx 10$$). Thirty-four years, just to get back to where you started. That's what real risk looks like.

## Losing less is a head start

In March 2020 the market had its worst crash since 2008. A portfolio that falls less in a crash also has less to climb back, so it gets back to its high sooner and starts compounding again before everyone else. That was my experience: I was diversified across sectors going into it, my portfolio fell a bit less than the S&P 500, and it recovered noticeably faster. A small gap on the way down becomes a head start on the way up. And the way compounding works, those head starts add up.

Everyone seems focused on finding the next big thing. The next Amazon, the next Tesla. Hardly anyone thinks about risk management. That ends up costing them money.

Risk management isn't only about protecting yourself from losses. It also makes you gain more.
