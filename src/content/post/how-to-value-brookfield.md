Nobody seems to know how to properly value a company like Brookfield. Which is why it stays undervalued, and why it remains such a good opportunity for those who do.

## Not one business, three engines

The first mistake with Brookfield is valuing it like a normal stock. A simple P/E isn't the right lens, because Brookfield isn't one business. It's three engines combined:

1. An alternative asset manager.
2. A wealth solutions platform built around insurance assets.
3. A set of operating businesses in infrastructure, renewables, private equity and real estate.

That's why the starting point should be distributable earnings, not reported net income. In 2025, Brookfield generated $5.4 billion of distributable earnings before realisations and $6.0 billion in total, while deployable capital hit a record $188 billion. Those numbers are much closer to owner earnings than GAAP profit, because they strip out some of the mark-to-market noise that can distort how the business is really doing.

## Engine 1: asset management

This is the cleanest piece. Fee-bearing capital rose 12% to $603 billion in 2025, and fee-related earnings climbed 22% to $3.0 billion. Brookfield's own share of distributable earnings from this segment was $1.9 billion, and management targets 15%+ annual growth in fee-related and distributable earnings over time.

Just as important, Brookfield's stake in Brookfield Asset Management was worth about $61.5 billion at year end, net of a non-recourse loan. That matters because it anchors the valuation to an asset with a market price, instead of a story. You're not being asked to believe in some vague conglomerate premium. You can already see one very large, very valuable piece.

## Engine 2: wealth solutions

This is where many investors stop doing the work. In 2025, Brookfield's wealth solutions business generated $1.7 billion of distributable earnings, grew insurance assets to $143 billion, and ended the year with $12.7 billion of book equity producing roughly $1.9 billion of annualised cash flow. Brookfield values that equity at about $28 billion.

You shouldn't take management's estimate as gospel. But you shouldn't dismiss it just because the structure is more complex than a plain insurer, either. The real question is whether this platform can keep compounding spread income and insurance float at attractive rates. If it can, the market is probably underrating a durable earnings stream simply because it sits inside a complicated wrapper.

## Engine 3: operating businesses

The operating businesses generated $1.6 billion of distributable earnings in 2025. These aren't random leftovers. They include high-quality renewable, infrastructure, private equity and real estate assets, with Brookfield reporting year-end occupancy of 96% in its "super core" real estate portfolio and 95% in its core-plus portfolio.

This is also where Brookfield's capital allocation record matters. In 2025 it completed about $91 billion of asset sales, deployed $126 billion of capital, and bought back more than $1 billion of its own shares. That doesn't prove the stock is cheap. But it shows Brookfield isn't a passive holding company waiting for markets to be kind. It's an active allocator that can sell, recycle, reinvest and repurchase across a very large asset base.

## The complexity discount

This is why the undervaluation argument is ultimately about a complexity discount. Investors prefer simple stories and clean comparisons. Brookfield offers neither. But complexity can be a source of mispricing.

A good recent example is the Boralex deal announced this week. Brookfield agreed, alongside La Caisse, to buy Boralex in a deal implying about C$9.0 billion of enterprise value. Brookfield will own 70%, and Boralex brings roughly 3,800 MW of wind, solar, hydro and storage assets, more than 90% of them under contract for an average of 10 years. That's classic Brookfield: buy a long-lived real-asset platform that public markets may not fully value, use scale and capital to grow it faster, and compound the cash flows.

## The right question

So the right question about Brookfield isn't "what multiple should I put on this year's earnings?" It's "what are these three engines worth separately, and how big a discount is the market applying because they sit together?"

Value the asset manager as a premium, compounding fee business. Give wealth solutions a sensible but conservative value. Then assign even a reasonable number to the operating businesses. Brookfield starts to look less like an expensive financial stock and more like a misunderstood collection of cash-flow machines.

That's the undervaluation case in a nutshell: Brookfield owns several strong businesses, but the market sees one complicated company, and prices it too cheaply as a result.
