## Prologue: read the subtext (November 2023)

In September 2023, Amazon announced it would invest up to $4B in Anthropic, a rival to the Microsoft-funded OpenAI. To the untrained eye, that looked like a big company diversifying into a promising startup so it could match Microsoft on the battlefield. But it wasn't that simple.

Anthropic is an AI research company. It needs an enormous amount of computing power in the cloud. Who has an enormous amount of computing power in the cloud? Amazon. As part of the deal, Anthropic made AWS its primary cloud provider. Google was doing the same thing, putting up to $2B more into the same startup, with Anthropic spending heavily on Google Cloud in return.

So this is Amazon sending billions to Anthropic so Anthropic can spend them back on Amazon. A weird loop, right?

Think of it from the cloud's side. Scaling up GPUs is expensive and risky. You build all that computing power and then pray someone rents it. There aren't many big AI players out there. If they don't pick you, you're stuck with a lot of idle graphics cards and a hole in your accounts. Or, if you're a bit smarter, you sign a partnership with a big model maker and guarantee the GPUs get used. If the bet goes badly, you still got years of paid bills and a proven record of hosting large training runs.

These deals were never really a bet on Anthropic itself. They were a bet on GPU usage going up, with a partner to fill the machines while they got built.

Two years later, that loop had become the whole industry.

## The AI money machine (October 2025)

Look at the AI landscape today and you'll see two gravity wells. OpenAI makes the models everyone wants. Nvidia makes the chips everyone needs. Around them, Microsoft, Oracle, AMD and Intel are desperate not to miss the party. Multi-billion-dollar commitments stack on top of each other. Everyone is funding everyone else's expansion.

It looks insane. It isn't. It's incentives.

The loop goes like this. Money buys chips and cloud capacity. Capacity trains models and runs apps. Apps bring usage and revenue. Revenue attracts more money. Every player funds the next turn, because every turn makes their own position harder to dislodge.

Each one wants something slightly different:

- **Nvidia** wants long, guaranteed orders, and its CUDA software on every machine so nobody can leave. It even invests in its own customers to pull demand forward.
- **The clouds** want their data centres full, with anchor tenants like OpenAI locked into long contracts.
- **The labs** want guaranteed compute at predictable prices, and more than one supplier so they have some leverage.
- **AMD and Intel** want one big, famous deployment that proves they're a real alternative to Nvidia. They'll discount, and even offer equity, to get it.

## What it does to the market

The good part is speed. Models and tools ship faster than ever, unit costs fall with scale, and there's money for hard bets like robotics and agents.

The bad part is that all this money pushes in one direction. If you've pre-paid for a mountain of GPUs, you'll favour designs that use a mountain of GPUs, even when a leaner approach exists. Credits and cross-shareholdings steer customers toward a partner's stack even when a neutral one is cheaper. And the more code is written for CUDA, the harder it gets to leave.

So we get blazing-fast progress, in a very narrow direction.

For Nvidia, it's great while it lasts, with the risk that supply catches up or a real alternative appears. For OpenAI, it buys speed at the cost of giant commitments that only make sense if it builds profitable products. For the clouds, it's growth, as long as the GPUs stay busy.

## What I'm watching

Three things tell you whether this loop holds up:

1. **Utilisation.** Are the reserved clusters actually busy, or sitting idle?
2. **Cost per token.** For the economics to work, it has to fall fast.
3. **Alternatives.** Google trains on its own TPUs, but almost everyone else depends on Nvidia. The loop is healthier if that changes.

Whether all this ends up good hangs on two things no contract can guarantee: real productivity gains, and enough competition to keep prices honest.

## Looking back from late 2026

Two of the three moved the way the loop needed. Cost per token fell fast: in mid-2026 OpenAI cut the price of its cheapest GPT-5.6 tier by 80% within weeks of launch. And non-CUDA stacks gained real ground, with Google's TPUs and Amazon's Trainium chips running frontier workloads, Anthropic's among them. Utilisation is still the one I can't see from the outside. It's the number I'd most like to know.
