## Prologue: read the subtext (November 2023)

In November 2023, Amazon announced it would invest $4B in Anthropic, a rival to the Microsoft-funded OpenAI. To the untrained eye, that looked like a big company diversifying into a promising startup so it could match Microsoft on the battlefield. But it wasn't that simple.

Anthropic is an AI research company. It needs an enormous amount of computing power in the cloud. Who has an enormous amount of computing power in the cloud? Amazon. As part of the deal, Anthropic made AWS its primary cloud provider. Google was doing the same thing, putting up to $2B more into the same startup, with Anthropic spending heavily on Google Cloud in return.

So this is Amazon sending billions to Anthropic so Anthropic can spend them back on Amazon. A weird loop, right?

Think of it from the cloud's side. Scaling up GPUs is expensive and risky. You build all that computing power and then pray someone rents it. There aren't many big AI players out there. If they don't pick you, you're stuck with a lot of idle graphics cards and a hole in your accounts. Or, if you're a bit smarter, you sign a partnership with a big model maker and guarantee the GPUs get used. If the bet goes badly, you still got years of paid bills and a proven record of hosting large training runs.

These deals were never really a bet on Anthropic itself. They were a bet on GPU usage going up, with a partner to fill the machines while they got built.

Two years later, that loop had become the whole industry.

## The AI money machine (October 2025)

Look at the AI landscape and you'll see something fascinating. Two gravity wells at the centre: OpenAI making the models everyone wants, and Nvidia making the chips everyone needs. Around them, Microsoft, Oracle, AMD and Intel are desperately trying to get in and not miss the party. Multi-billion dollar commitments stack on top of each other. Everyone is funding everyone else's expansion.

Looks insane. But it's not insanity. It's just incentives.

### The loop in one line

Capital buys GPUs and cloud capacity. That capacity trains models and powers apps. Those apps generate usage and revenue. That revenue attracts more capital. Everyone funds the next turn because each turn entrenches their position further.

### What does each player want?

**Nvidia** wants multi-year volume commitments for GPUs and networking gear. It wants CUDA everywhere: sticky and irreplaceable. It co-invests with customers to pull demand forward and starve competitors.

**The clouds** want to fill data centres with high-margin AI workloads. They want anchor tenants like OpenAI locked in with credits, reserved capacity and long contracts.

**OpenAI and the other labs** want priority access to compute and power at predictable prices, while diversifying suppliers to gain some leverage (mostly Nvidia and AMD). They also seed downstream startups that find fresh, creative ways to use their models, so demand for the models keeps going up.

**AMD and Intel** want to break Nvidia's lead with aggressive pricing, software support and even equity sweeteners. They need flagship deployments to prove their stacks are viable alternatives.

**Startups and VCs** trade neutrality for speed. Subsidised compute now, lock-in later.

### How the deal web changes the game

Big pre-buys and reservations push everyone to use more compute to justify their commitments. That favours compute-hungry designs over leaner alternatives.

Lock-in hardens. Cross-equity stakes, credits and exclusive features tilt choices toward a partner's stack, even when neutral options are cheaper.

Pricing gets distorted. Subsidies and bundles make it harder for independents to compete on pure price-performance.

Power becomes strategy. Multi-gigawatt plans force power purchase agreements, new sites and energy deals. Activity concentrates in a few regions.

Standards freeze. The more code lives on CUDA or on a single cloud, the higher the switching costs, and the wider the incumbents' moat.

### What does this mean for the businesses?

For Nvidia, short-term everything looks rosy: backlogs, pricing power, ecosystem gravity. Long-term, there are risks: concentration, regulators, and a margin squeeze if supply normalises or credible alternatives take share.

For OpenAI, it's good for access and speed. The cost is dependency and giant take-or-pay commitments that have to turn into profitable products. They need profitable products.

For the clouds, it's good for growth and for the narrative. It's risky if tenants don't keep the GPUs hot and utilisation falls short of the massive capex.

For AMD and Intel, it's a big opportunity if they can turn small wins into a real developer ecosystem. Right now they're somewhat outside the big boys' club. Software maturity and slow migration off CUDA are working against them.

### What does this mean for the AI market?

A rapid innovation cadence. New models and tooling ship faster. Scale drives down unit costs over time. There's more capital for hard bets like robotics, multimodal systems and agents. All of that is good.

But we're also seeing massive concentration and higher barriers to entry. Lock-in that can slow down open alternatives. Demand distortion, where cheap credits push the field toward GPU-intensive approaches even when leaner ones exist.

The result is blazing-fast iteration now, but in a very narrow direction. Long-run competition depends on whether viable non-Nvidia stacks and open-weight models can stay relevant.

### What to watch

We need to keep an eye on utilisation of reserved clusters. Are they actually getting used, or are they sitting idle? For this party to go on, economies of scale have to kick in, so we should see the cost per token falling fast. And we should see *some* ecosystem diversity. Please, someone train a model without CUDA. Alternatives need to gain traction.

### Conclusion

The AI money machine runs on aligned incentives. Each player finances the next turn of a loop that converts capital into compute, compute into products, and products back into capital. It's excellent for the leaders while the flywheel spins. It also concentrates power and bends the market's choices.

Whether this ends up good overall hangs on two things no contract can guarantee: durable productivity gains, and real competition to keep prices honest and innovation broad.

## Looking back from late 2026

Two of the three signals moved the way the loop needed. Cost per token fell fast: in mid-2026 OpenAI cut the price of its cheapest GPT-5.6 tier by 80% within weeks of launch. And non-CUDA stacks gained real ground, with Google's TPUs and Amazon's Trainium chips running frontier workloads, Anthropic's among them. Utilisation of reserved clusters is still the one I can't see from the outside. It's the number I'd most like to know.
