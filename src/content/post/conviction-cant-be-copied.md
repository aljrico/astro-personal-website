## We spend too much time reading what other people think

I think we spend way too much time reading about and weighing up what other people do in investing, and too little time on introspection. The person you most need to understand to be a good investor is yourself. It's not enough to know what Warren Buffett or Peter Lynch would do.

We also live in a Google Search world. People wrongly believe that if they search long enough, they'll find the answer to every question. What they should do more often is stop and think the question through. Consider what it means and what it assumes. Sometimes the most important thing is working out which questions actually matter. The process of figuring out an answer can be more enlightening than the answer itself.

We need to think through our own assumptions about markets. We need to understand why companies do what they do, rather than focusing on what other people think about those companies.

Good investors need to own their investment philosophy.

**Conviction can't be copied. It can't be emulated.** You have to earn it with reflection and consideration, through sweat and tears and blood. Then, and only then, will you be convinced of what you bought and why you can trust your portfolio.

Only real conviction stops us from doing stupid things when the markets go wild.

We all like to pretend emotions don't affect us. But they do. It's natural to get scared when the market drops sharply. It's natural to feel overly optimistic after a win. And we all wonder whether it's time to sell when a stock jumps 10% in a day.

It's hard not to let those emotions drive our behaviour. It always is. People who pretend otherwise are fooling themselves. We need to be aware of it so we can take measures against it. For example:

- **Don't check the market every day.** Whatever decision you made shouldn't change in a day, and it shouldn't change because of price movements.
- **Only invest in things you know deeply.** When bad news starts flooding in (it always does), you'll be better prepared to throw out the useless noise.
- **Add money on a schedule.** I add funds to my investment account every month, on the same day at the same hour, the moment my paycheck comes in. That way no recent price move or random headline affects the decision. Otherwise I'd be tempted to "wait for things to calm down" or "wait until something gets cheaper", and I'd be fooling myself.

Know your limitations first. Only then can you master them.

## The biggest mistake I keep making

Most people believe the biggest mistake an investor can make is holding an asset all the way into bankruptcy. I disagree. The biggest mistake investors make, again and again, is selling a stock that could have grown tenfold.

They spot a winner, take a bit of profit, and chicken out. Holding is the hardest part of investing. When you're right about something, don't get off the train while it's still rolling. Sit still and enjoy the ride.

As Peter Lynch put it, selling your winners and holding your losers is like cutting the flowers and watering the weeds.

Between you and me, I'm not innocent of this. I've made this mistake many times, and I keep learning from it. As of November 2021, when I first wrote this list:

- **Tesla.** I sold in 2020 after a 10x run. If I'd held one more year, it would have been 25x.
- **Bitcoin.** I discovered it in 2016. I bought some and told all my friends to do the same. Then I sold everything in 2017 after a 15x run. By November 2021, that would have been 165x.
- **Google.** Back in 2012, when I started playing around with stocks, I was in love with Google and its culture. I dreamed about working there, and it kept me going through university. I figured that if working there was so attractive, the most talented people on the planet would push each other to join, and that would make Google the smartest company ever. So I put almost all my savings, $500 at the time, into it. It doubled in about a year, so I sold everything, cashed out $1,000 and considered myself a genius. Had I held, those $500 would have been worth more than $5,000 by late 2021.

Yes, I've dodged a couple of bullets along the way. But at what cost?

## Why good analysts are terrible investors

We've all seen them. Long pieces of analysis. Lots of ideas. Extensive research on the future of certain markets. Theoretical experts in hot sectors. And still, when they put their own money on the line, they lose. Something seems off, right? Maybe they're fooling everyone. Maybe these "experts" don't have that much expertise.

The truth is they might be right about everything and still fail. Because of poor execution.

Making money in the stock market isn't only about brains. It's also about guts. My rule of thumb, and it is just my opinion, is that successful investing is something like 90% emotional mastery and 10% intellect.

Not many people accept this. They think theoretical knowledge will be enough. It isn't. You need to master your own psychology.

Success comes to those with the temperament to HOLD through bad times. To buy even when there's blood in the streets. To buy even when every headline is screaming that the end is near and a recession is coming.

Plenty of people panic-sold Apple at the end of 2018, after writing article after article about the company's bright future. Hordes of analysts sold Google when COVID first hit in March 2020. They all said, "I'll buy back later, when things calm down."

I've seen many great analysts make terrible mistakes because they couldn't hold it together.

The average Joe blindly buying an S&P 500 or Nasdaq 100 index fund every month is beating the vast majority of analysts. Not because he knows *more*. Because he knows *better*.
