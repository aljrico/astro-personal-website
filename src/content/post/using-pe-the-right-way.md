Are tech stocks in a bubble? Is the market about to crash? Is Tesla really that overvalued? Could Amazon be the cheapest tech company right now?

Today I'll look at how P/E is often misused, and how to understand it well enough to make it useful.

## A static number

P/E is arguably the most misunderstood multiple of all time. It sounds so simple. A company is a machine that produces money. The more money it makes (earnings), the more we should be willing to pay for it. So the price-to-earnings ratio looks like the obvious choice: buy companies with a low P/E, sell the ones with a high P/E.

But that's a very static view. Take an easy example. Company A makes $20 of earnings per share today, and company B makes $18. But B has a more ambitious plan and expects to grow earnings by about 15% a year. A is more conservative and expects 7% a year.

So right now, A makes more money. But B will probably be making more within a couple of years.

In real life, this leads to B having a higher P/E than A, and two opposite reactions. Some people believe B's plan is doable and are happy to pay a higher P/E for it. Others think B's plan can't be done, so its P/E looks too high and they call it "too expensive".

## Some real companies

Here are the P/E ratios of a few big names everyone argues about:

| Company | P/E |
| --- | --- |
| Amazon | 42 |
| Apple | 26.5 |
| Google | 21 |
| Microsoft | 25 |
| Tesla | 89 |

Before jumping to conclusions, let's talk about expected growth. P/E only reflects current earnings, not future ones. Here's how analysts expected each company to grow its earnings, based on the companies' own guidance:

| Company | Expected annual growth |
| --- | --- |
| Amazon | 30-50% |
| Apple | 6-8% |
| Google | 5-16% |
| Microsoft | 14-18% |
| Tesla | 25-46% |

Expectations about the future are always uncertain. Keep that in mind.

## PEG

How do you value that? You divide the P/E by the growth rate. That's pretty standard, and it's called the price/earnings-to-growth ratio, or PEG:

| Company | PEG |
| --- | --- |
| Amazon | 0.85-1.4 |
| Apple | 3.3-4.4 |
| Google | 1.3-4.2 |
| Microsoft | 1.4-1.8 |
| Tesla | 1.9-3.6 |

All of this assumes the analysts' growth expectations are right.

So many people are shouting about how "irrationally" expensive Tesla is. But is it? By PEG it looks far more reasonable than Apple, for example.

Am I saying you should buy Amazon because it has the lowest PEG? Not really.

## Study the business

What I'm trying to stress is how much growth expectations matter. It all comes down to this: how will the company perform in the years to come? Will Amazon grow earnings by 30-50% a year? If you believe so, Amazon is a bargain right now. If you don't, it probably isn't.

So how do you know which company will grow its earnings? My friend, here's the hard truth: you can't know for sure. The best you can do is study the business. Not the ratios. Not the financial statements. Not the shape of the price chart.

You have to study the business itself.

Will Apple grow its earnings? Only if it sells more iPhones, or keeps making great products that bring more people into the ecosystem. So to analyse it, you start by researching how phones and tablets are made. What advantage does Apple have that others can't copy? Can it keep it? Is it opening new markets, like it did with AirPods?

Will Tesla grow its earnings? Only if it sells more cars. Is its battery technology better than the competition's? Will its AI prove as useful as it hopes? Could Toyota suddenly show up with a better electric car?

These are the questions to ask yourself before you invest in these companies.

The only way to make sense of the numbers is to understand their context. It doesn't matter how refined and complicated your calculations are. Without context, they mean nothing.

## Looking back

Amazon, the cheapest company in my PEG table, lost money in 2022. A $2.7 billion net loss, its first annual loss since 2014, mostly from writing down its stake in Rivian. So much for 30-50% earnings growth.

That's the point of this post, made for me by reality. The PEG looked great because the growth estimate looked great. The estimate was wrong. The ratio was only ever as good as the guess underneath it.
