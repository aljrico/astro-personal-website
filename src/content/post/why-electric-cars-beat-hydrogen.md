I'd been answering questions about electric versus hydrogen cars here and there, so I decided to collect my thoughts in one place.

## Efficiency

The most efficient way to turn energy into movement is with electricity. Every other option loses way too much along the way (think of steam engines). So hydrogen is a way to **store** electricity, not to use it.

The idea is to put hydrogen tanks and fuel cells in cars, and refill them at hydrogen stations. First you use electricity to split water and produce the hydrogen. Then you compress it into high-pressure tanks, which uses a lot of energy too. Then you transport it to a station. Finally, the car's fuel cell turns it back into electricity, which is another loss.

How much energy is lost along the way? A lot. Studies of the full chain from power source to wheels typically find that battery-electric cars use roughly [three to four times less energy per kilometre](https://theconversation.com/why-battery-powered-vehicles-stack-up-better-than-hydrogen-106844) than hydrogen fuel cell cars. Even if the technology improves, wouldn't it be easier to just make everything electric and skip the hydrogen middleman?

## Infrastructure

It's not only that. Hydrogen cars also need completely separate infrastructure. To supply hydrogen stations, we'd need a network of hydrogen pipes. We have electric grids everywhere, but not hydrogen pipes. Imagine how costly and slow it would be to lay them everywhere. Or, more realistically, we'd need a fleet of trucks carrying hydrogen tanks to every station. The investment required would be monstrous. Unless hydrogen turns out to have a massive advantage over batteries, nobody is going to pay for it.

## Weight and storage

So does hydrogen have a big advantage over batteries? I doubt it.

Hydrogen packs a lot of energy for its weight. At first glance that seems ideal for cars. We all know from Tesla and NIO presentations that weight is one of the main limits on building efficient cars, and batteries are heavy. That's why they focus so much on battery chemistry and materials. If hydrogen is so light, shouldn't hydrogen cars be lighter? Isn't that the big advantage we're looking for?

Unfortunately, it's really the opposite. Yes, hydrogen is very light. It's the lightest element in the periodic table. It's so small and light that it slips out of almost any container you can think of, like trying to catch smoke with your bare hands. So the tanks need extra layers to keep it in, and they have to hold it at enormous pressure. Those tanks are expensive to make, complicated to handle, and heavy. That basically wipes out hydrogen's advantage.

## Safety

Even if all that were solved by some future technology, hydrogen is extremely flammable, much more than petrol, and it leaks easily. Accidents happen: in June 2019 a hydrogen station near Oslo exploded, and Toyota and Hyundai temporarily paused fuel cell car sales in Norway afterwards. So carmakers have to compensate with thick, heavy, carefully engineered tanks, which makes the cars even more complicated, heavier and more expensive.

## The verdict

In theory, hydrogen cars look like the perfect replacement for combustion engines. In practice, hydrogen is very expensive to make, store and move around. So expensive that hydrogen cars don't add up with today's technology. Maybe someday humanity will make it work efficiently, but we can't afford to wait that long. Climate change is here and now. The demand for alternatives to petrol engines is here and now. Not next century, not next decade. Now.

No, I don't think hydrogen cars are the future. Not the near future, at least. I actually see them as diverting resources from solutions we can use today. Toyota and Honda might take a while to accept it, but the evidence is overwhelming.

Electric cars are the way to go.
