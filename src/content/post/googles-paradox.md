If ten years ago you had asked ANYONE to bet on the one company that would invent AI, they'd have said Google. Not Amazon or Microsoft or even Meta. And definitely not Apple. The only realistic bet was Google.

And to be completely honest, they kind of did. I like to remind everyone that the "T" in ChatGPT stands for Transformer, a machine-learning architecture developed by researchers at Google Brain and Google Research and published in 2017 in their now-famous paper "Attention Is All You Need". It flew under the radar for almost everyone, even for many of the people who developed it, until OpenAI picked it up and built what could become one of humanity's greatest inventions.

How come Google, out of freaking anyone, missed this opportunity?

## Research, product, ecosystem

Developing technological breakthroughs that later become industry-changing products is kind of their thing. It's their modus operandi. They take a hard research idea, turn it into software, and then into a business. You could summarise the entire history of the company in three steps. Publish a breakthrough. Build the product that only that breakthrough makes possible. Let an ecosystem bloom around it. That ecosystem just so happens to protect their core business, or at least lets them rent picks and shovels to everyone else.

Case in point: **PageRank**. A revolutionary way of ranking pages by their links. You all know the story by now. Search engines used to suck. They were dumb, shallow and easy to game. Until Sergey Brin and Larry Page, then PhD students at Stanford, published PageRank in 1998 and immediately built Google Search on top of it.

They also revolutionised the crunching of huge datasets with **MapReduce**, a programming model for reliably splitting work across thousands of machines. It let them process the entire web on cheap commodity servers. When they published it, it helped birth the whole "big data" industry. It hid the complexity of distributed systems and let ordinary engineers use warehouse-scale computers.

They also defined the Web 2.0 era. Back in the 90s, most websites were plain HTML pages, and every user action loaded a whole new page from the server. Google didn't invent the technique that changed this. The browser plumbing for it, XMLHttpRequest, came from Microsoft, and the name **Ajax** ("Asynchronous JavaScript and XML") was coined in 2005. But Gmail and Google Maps were the products that showed everyone what it could do: a page that fetches data in the background and updates only the parts that need updating.

They made statistical, and later neural, machine translation mainstream with Google Translate. They let barely technical users dabble in machine learning with TensorFlow. And Kubernetes, which started inside Google, became a backbone of modern cloud infrastructure.

I think you get the point. Each paper solved a problem. Each solution became an industry. This is how their moat was compounding. These breakthroughs enabled products with huge network effects (Search, Maps, Docs, YouTube and so on). That created a virtuous cycle. The research attracts talent, the talent writes the next paper, and the flywheel turns.

## So what went wrong with Transformers?

Why didn't Google ship the first ChatGPT, the product that, once again, revolutionised internet technology?

Google published the Transformer paper in June 2017. Its researchers soon used the architecture to generate Wikipedia-style articles, and it worked remarkably well. Then they moved on to other problems. Just one year later, OpenAI published its first Generative Pre-trained Transformer, or GPT. Google did build BERT, a Transformer model that was good at understanding language, not writing it. It went into Search in 2019 to better grasp the intent and nuance of queries. Nothing more. That same year, OpenAI released GPT-2. In 2020, GPT-3. And then, in November 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT, and the world changed overnight.

How did Google miss this? They had every advantage. The technology. The data. The computing power. The talent.

Part of the answer is **risk tolerance**. Google had a search business to protect. Billions in revenue. Regulatory scrutiny. Brand reputation. Bad AI output could damage everything. OpenAI had nothing to lose.

Part of it is **focus**. Google researchers chase interesting problems, publish papers and move on to the next challenge. OpenAI had one goal, artificial general intelligence, and every project aimed there. Anything else was a distraction.

And part of it is a **religion of speed**. Google debates, committees review, legal approves, products get polished. OpenAI ships. ChatGPT had flaws, and users didn't care. Perfection is the enemy of revolution.

## The paradox

Put those three together and you get the paradox. The same things that made Google the best research lab in the world, a protected cash machine, freedom to chase any interesting problem, and the patience to get things right, are exactly what stopped it from shipping the product its own research made possible. OpenAI didn't out-invent Google. It out-shipped it, using Google's invention.

Google has spent the years since catching up, and catching up fast. But as I wrote in January, in [Google Is Winning a Losing War](/posts/google-is-winning-a-losing-war/), winning this race may still cost Google a lot of what made Search such a good business in the first place.
