Eating an indecent amount of Nutella doesn't make me an expert in cocoa futures or chocolate production. But it does spark my interest when prices start creeping up. Because, in case you haven't noticed, the price of chocolate is through the roof and still climbing. A tonne of cocoa passed $10,000 a few days ago. It has roughly quadrupled in two years and is now more expensive than a tonne of copper. Soon we'll have to hand out actual fruit at Halloween, and I can't think of anything scarier than that.

So what's going on?

## Côte d'Ivoire

Côte d'Ivoire is the world's top producer of cocoa beans, with around 40% of the planet's supply on its own. And it's facing hotter-than-usual temperatures, which means drier-than-usual soil. That would be bad enough by itself. But the weather has been so erratic that it has also brought massive rains, which wash away the soil's fertility.

Massive rains don't rehydrate soil after a dry season. You need steady, moderate rain. Massive rains cause floods. And floods are made far worse by deforestation, which is exactly what the country has been through. Côte d'Ivoire has lost most of its forest cover over the past decades, much of it cleared for cocoa. In some regions, up to 80% of protected forest has been cleared for cocoa farms. Without dense forest, heavy rain turns into massive floods that wash away nutrients and rot the existing plantations.

## Ghana, and a virus

Ghana, the second-largest producer and Côte d'Ivoire's neighbour, isn't doing much better. Together, the two countries produce more than 60% of the world's cocoa beans. Same weather, same problem.

And then there's the part I missed when I first wrote this: disease. Wet, flooded fields spread black pod, a fungus that rots the pods. Worse is cocoa swollen shoot virus, spread by tiny insects called mealybugs. It stunts the trees and eventually kills them, and there's no cure. You have to cut the tree down and replant, and a new tree takes years to produce. In 2024 Ghana's cocoa board reported that 81% of the crop in its main growing region was affected. On top of that, illegal gold mining has been eating into Ghana's cocoa farms.

The result is a global shortfall of hundreds of thousands of tonnes of cocoa beans this season. Grinders and chocolate makers are scrambling for whatever beans they can find, and prices explode.

## Too many eggs in one basket

The deeper problem is concentration. Most of the world's cocoa comes from one small corner of West Africa, grown on small farms by farmers who see only part of the world price and can't afford to replant. When that corner has a bad year, the whole world has a bad year.

From a ship stuck in the Suez Canal to COVID shutting down logistics, we keep learning how fragile supply chains are when they depend on one place.

Chocolate is just the tastiest example.

## Looking back

Prices kept climbing, past $12,000 a tonne in December 2024, before high prices did what high prices do. Farmers elsewhere planted more. Ecuador, where farmers get most of the world price and grow a variety bred to resist swollen shoot, ramped up so fast that it was on course to overtake Ghana as the world's second-largest producer. By late 2025 prices had fallen by about a third, still far above where they started.
