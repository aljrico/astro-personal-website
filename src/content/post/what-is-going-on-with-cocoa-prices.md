Eating an indecent amount of Nutella doesn't make me an expert in cocoa futures or chocolate production. But it does spark my interest when prices start creeping up. Because, in case you haven't noticed, the price of chocolate is through the roof and still climbing. A tonne of cocoa passed $10,000 a few days ago. It has roughly quadrupled in two years and is now more expensive than a tonne of copper. Soon we'll have to hand out actual fruit at Halloween, and I can't think of anything scarier than that.

So what's going on?

## Côte d'Ivoire

Côte d'Ivoire is the world's top producer of cocoa beans, with around 40% of the planet's supply on its own. And it's facing hotter-than-usual temperatures, which means drier-than-usual soil. That would be bad enough by itself. But the weather has been so erratic that it has also brought massive rains, which wash away the soil's fertility.

Massive rains don't rehydrate soil after a dry season. You need steady, moderate rain. Massive rains cause floods. And floods are made far worse by deforestation, which is exactly what the country has been through. Côte d'Ivoire has lost most of its forest cover over the past decades, much of it cleared for cocoa. In some regions, up to 80% of protected forest has been cleared for cocoa farms. Without dense forest, heavy rain turns into massive floods that wash away nutrients and rot the existing plantations.

## Ghana and the rest

Ghana, the second-largest producer and Côte d'Ivoire's neighbour, isn't doing much better. Most of the same problems apply. Together, the two countries produce more than 60% of the world's cocoa beans, and both are dealing with political upheaval and climate disasters. That alone would catastrophically disrupt supply.

But there's more. Years of record temperatures are scrambling climate patterns. El Niño, for instance, is hitting unusually hard this time, and that's affecting the other major cocoa producers in Central and South America too.

The result is a global shortfall of hundreds of thousands of tonnes of cocoa beans this season. Production is down, the supply chain is in shambles, and cocoa grinders and the rest of the industry are scrambling for whatever beans they can find, squeezing supply further and making prices explode.

## Fragile supply chains

This isn't the first time in recent years we've seen a supply chain catastrophically disrupted. From a ship stuck in the Suez Canal to COVID shutting down logistics, we're learning the hard way how fragile supply chains are, and how big the impact is when they break.

Chocolate is just the tastiest example.
