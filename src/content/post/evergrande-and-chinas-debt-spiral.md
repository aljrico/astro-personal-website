Is Evergrande the new Lehman Brothers? Is it the first domino that finally breaks the economy?

## Who is Evergrande?

In a nutshell, a huge Chinese real estate developer. How huge? Around $75 billion of annual revenue. More than 1,300 projects in some 280 cities.

The core of the business is building buildings. Massive buildings. Each project can take months or years and needs obscene amounts of cash. So much cash that developers can't fund it all themselves. It's normal for them to raise money from many sources for big projects. That spreads the risk and allows more ambitious ventures.

The more projects a developer takes on at once, the more risk it faces. What if buyers pull out? What if a project fails and brings in less cash than expected? That's why developers normally limit how many projects they run at once. To control risk.

But Evergrande was always unusually ambitious, taking on project after project. Why not? Other people were carrying the financial risk.

How? Easy. Debt. The cause of, and solution to, all of life's problems. Evergrande became the most indebted property developer on planet Earth, with more than $300 billion in total liabilities.

That debt let it bid far above market prices for land and price out its rivals. The aggressive strategy brought massive success, but with a side effect: the debt skyrocketed.

Who financed all this? Mostly banks. But a lot of the money also came from working-class families paying upfront for their dream home.

## How debt spirals work

However indebted a company is, it can keep things under control as long as profits keep growing. Debt has to be repaid with interest. The longer you delay, the more interest you pay. And if the interest ever exceeds your profits, it's game over.

When debt goes up, so do interest payments. And interest eats into profits. So the very profits you need to repay the debt are being nibbled away by the interest on that debt. It's a vicious cycle.

The problem with debt is that everything looks fine until it suddenly isn't. There's a tipping point where profits can no longer cover the interest. Once that happens, things go south fast. Confidence collapses, investors run, and the debt can kill the company in one sweep.

Evergrande reached that point in 2020. It simply couldn't keep up with its interest payments, and everyone knew it. That summer, a letter reportedly sent by Evergrande to the Guangdong government leaked, warning of a cash crunch and the risk to the wider financial system. Evergrande said it was fake. Some read it not as a warning but as a threat.

You know the saying. If you owe the bank $1 million, you have a problem. If you owe the bank $1 billion, the bank has a problem. And if your company owes $300 billion to banks, suppliers and homebuyers all over a country, maybe the country has a problem.

Evergrande got through 2020, partly by squeezing everyone around it: employees were reportedly pressured to lend it money or buy its investment products to keep their bonuses. And, to the shock of no one, it used the breathing room to take on even more risk and more debt. In September 2021 it ran out of road again, and markets around the world wobbled.

## A million homes unfinished

The situation is now so bad that Evergrande can't even finish the projects it has started. More than a million buyers are waiting for homes they've already paid for. If Evergrande collapses, many of them lose their savings and their home.

This time there's no room for games. The message to the Chinese authorities is clear: save the company again, or watch a big chunk of your financial system take the hit. Bail out the excesses of a greedy corporation, or watch a million working-class homebuyers get ruined.

What will the Chinese government do? Nobody knows. I don't envy them. They're in a really tough spot.

My guess is they'll find a legal workaround to allow "flexible" debt repayments that keep the company alive, while tightening control over its management. And even if they don't, I don't see the credit crunch spreading outside China. So I wouldn't worry about the global economy.

## Looking back

I got the second half right and the first half wrong. Beijing didn't rescue Evergrande. It defaulted on its offshore debt in December 2021, a Hong Kong court ordered it liquidated in January 2024, and its shares were delisted in 2025. The government's priority turned out to be getting the unfinished homes built, not saving the company. The crunch did hit China's whole property sector hard, but it didn't turn into a global financial crisis.
