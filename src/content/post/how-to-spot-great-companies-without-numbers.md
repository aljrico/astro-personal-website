Most investors start their research by crunching ratios and formulas. I don't think that's a good idea, even if the goal is to calculate a "fair price". There are hundreds of thousands of companies out there. Are you going to calculate the perfect price for all of them? No. You'll narrow the list down first. Most people simply take the biggest companies in the US (the S&P 500 or the Nasdaq 100) and go from there.

I suggest you step out of the crowd and do this instead. First, build a small-ish list of great companies. Forget about metrics and ratios and focus on the business. You could buy them all, hold them, and probably get above-average returns anyway. Only once you have that list should you start playing with ratios and formulas.

Alright, no more talk. Here are the patterns to look for.

## Recurring revenue

Simply put, money that comes in regularly, every month or every year. A company with recurring revenue can expand it later as customers buy more products. Think software licences or subscriptions.

Netflix and Oracle are good examples.

## Friendly middlemen

Which products can only be delivered through professional middlemen? The first example that comes to mind is plumbers. Let's be real: you're not going to buy materials for your drains or your cistern yourself. You hire a plumber, who does the work and picks and buys the materials, because you trust their judgement. If plumbers have a brand they trust, they'll always recommend it.

If a company has won the confidence of the professionals in its industry, that almost guarantees present and future customers. The same happens with contact lenses and dental implants, but also with industrial installations and software.

3M and Geberit are good examples. But Microsoft is my favourite one.

## Toll roads

Some services are almost unavoidable. Whatever you want to do, you have to use them, and every time you do, you pay a fee. Toll roads and ports are the everyday examples, but some companies hold niches that work the same way.

Rating agencies like S&P Global are the perfect example, and so are payment networks like Visa and Mastercard.

## Low prices

Cutting prices to win customers is a common strategy, but it eats into profits. It's a short-term fix with a clear limit: you can't cut below your margin without losing money. But if your competitors are cutting prices and you're not particularly special, you have no choice, or you'll lose customers.

Some businesses, though, have such wide margins, or such low costs, that they can drop prices further than the competition can follow without losing money. Those businesses can obliterate their competition. A company with a sustainable price advantage holds tremendous power.

Ryanair has done this for short-haul flights, and Costco has been disrupting retail with it.

## Pricing power

Cutting prices can win you customers, while raising them can push customers away. But not always. Some companies can raise prices without hurting demand. That is just huge.

Luxury brands like LVMH and Hermès are the obvious examples. But Apple and Microsoft hold this kind of power too.

## Brand strength

Simply put, a company that creates value just by being well known. Such is the power of a strong brand.

McDonald's and Coca-Cola are probably the most obvious examples.

## Innovation dominance

It's not only about money, but it's largely about money. Some companies have the culture, the motivation and the capital to out-research everyone else. Not just to improve existing products, but to create products that open new markets.

When I first wrote this in 2021, my examples were Tesla and Novo Nordisk. Both have since shown how fragile this kind of moat is. Tesla's lead in electric cars shrank as the rest of the industry caught up. Novo created the GLP-1 obesity market and then watched Eli Lilly overtake it. An innovation lead is a moat only for as long as you keep it.

## Cost to replicate

Some businesses are just harder than others. Opening a café is much simpler than building a chip factory. It's unfair, but such is life. Naturally, you see far more people opening cafés than factories. Which means if you own a café, you're much more exposed to disruption. You don't have a moat.

Businesses like ASML or TSMC are so hard to replicate that they essentially hold monopolies. Airbus and Boeing are another: they have shared the market for large passenger jets for decades, and even Boeing's years of costly mistakes haven't let a serious new rival in. That tells you how high the wall is.

## How moats are created and destroyed

I've been surprised to find that many people see competitive advantages as something inherently bad. They argue that a company with a huge moat has more room to squeeze every customer. The user is trapped using a company's products against their will. They don't feel valued, they feel ripped off, and the company gets away with it because there's no realistic alternative.

Some companies do use their advantages to squeeze customers. And I believe that is always a mistake, regardless of morality.

Some people wrongly think of moats as static. Moats change and evolve. They can grow, widen and get stronger. But they can also erode and fade away if they're taken for granted. Because taking your customers for granted is the surest way to destroy a company's moat.

It's a very stupid thing for a company to mistreat its customers. They pay the bills. They make the company valuable. When a company focuses on the value it can extract instead of the value it can provide, it's a sign it has lost its way.

Raise prices unreasonably, or impose frustrating conditions, and you create unhappy customers. Unhappy customers actively look for alternatives, and sooner or later they find them. Satisfied customers stay content and loyal.

Traditional companies mistreating their customers is exactly what created the openings for Netflix, Uber and Square.

Despite all its shortcomings, the main reason Amazon is a successful business has always been its extreme focus on customer satisfaction. It can get away with other things as long as its customers are happy. But no company is immune to this, not even the biggest fish. The moment some of Amazon's customers got frustrated, alternatives like Shopify and MercadoLibre started popping up everywhere.

**A company's advantage is defined not by the value it can extract from its customers, but by the value it can provide to them.**

And that is how moats are created and destroyed.
