*Originally posted on [eToro](https://www.etoro.com/posts/65f1ed80-5310-11ee-8080-80003a4eb433) on 14 September 2023, two days after Unity announced the fee.*

Unity is changing its pricing. In a nutshell, it's adding a fee of up to $0.20 for every install of a game or app made with Unity. This is **on top** of the subscription fees it already charges.

I don't want to argue about Unity raising prices. That's a very natural thing to do, whether developers like it or not. But this specific way of calculating fees is, in my honest opinion, a grievous mistake. Because it destroys trust.

## Fixed costs, unlimited upside

A huge number of companies have been built with their whole business plan structured around certain expected costs. For games and apps with 3D experiences, the engine is a major one. Not only how **much** you pay, but how those costs scale.

If your business has fixed costs but unlimited potential income, you have what's called **asymmetric risk**. Asymmetric risk is good, because it eases investors' fears. With fixed costs, you can predict pretty accurately how much you'll lose if everything goes badly. And since income is uncapped, the sky's the limit. Under those conditions, it's much easier to convince investors to take big risks.

This new pricing breaks that balance.

Imagine a real scenario. You make a game that scales massively but isn't well monetised. Maybe the free tier is too generous, or the premium offer isn't enticing enough. Whatever the reason, you get a lot of users but your average revenue per user (ARPU) isn't great. Under the old pricing, that's disappointing. Maybe even unprofitable. Not great, but manageable. You have an unprofitable business with fixed costs, so you can do the maths and work out how long until you run out of cash. Can you pivot in time? Should you pull the plug and cut your losses? Maybe scaling up will fix it. You have good enough information to decide.

Under the new pricing? You're ruined. You're paying per god damn install. If you double your user base, you double your costs. The risk profile of your little adventure is now wildly different. You can't count on economies of scale to save you. Investors will predictably be less keen to give you money. So game design will get less adventurous.

## Unity is burning its own moat

That's bad for players, but it's actually terrible for Unity too. Fewer risky ventures mean fewer huge hits. Let's be honest, most wildly creative new games flop. But some don't. And when they succeed, the studio stays with the engine that made them successful **forever**. They have to maintain that game, release a sequel, or try an even more creative idea. Which engine will they use, other than the one they already know? That's a massive moat. And Unity is, ironically, burning it down with this move.

## And Apple gets hurt too

You know who else gets badly hurt by this obviously rushed decision? Apple.

Wait, what? Hang on. In June 2023 Apple announced its most ambitious product in years, the Vision Pro. It could change the world or become an embarrassing flop. Honestly, there's no middle ground. Most of that comes down to how much gets built for it. Apple has placed **a lot** of trust in developers using existing engines to build cool 3D stuff. And Unity is, **by far**, the most popular engine for that kind of work. The timing could not be worse.

Maybe Unity shoots itself in the foot and developers migrate to Unreal. It'll take them a while to get good enough to churn out experimental new VR experiences.

And even if I'm wrong, Apple still gets hurt. Maybe I'm exaggerating, and developers will just complain for a few months and get back to work. Even then, there'll be less excitement and less experimentation, and less time spent on cute little side projects and highly experimental ones. Exactly the kind of projects that should be going to the Vision Pro.

**So here's the hot take**: this is bad for developers. It's worse for players. It's probably terrible for Unity. And it will definitely hurt the Vision Pro's chances.

## Epilogue

Unity [walked back most of the fee on 22 September 2023](https://www.reuters.com/technology/unity-software-rolls-back-parts-new-pricing-policy-after-backlash-2023-09-22/), ten days after announcing it. CEO John Riccitiello stepped down in October. A year later, on 12 September 2024, Unity [cancelled the Runtime Fee completely](https://unity.com/blog/unity-is-canceling-the-runtime-fee) and went back to subscription price rises instead.

The Vision Pro did turn out to be closer to the flop end of the spectrum. Sales stayed weak, and by 2026 Apple had [reportedly paused further development](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/04/29/apple-vision-pro-m5-flop/) after an M5 refresh that didn't move the needle.
