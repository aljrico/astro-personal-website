The hottest debate in mobility investing isn't about Uber's take rates. It's about whether autonomous vehicles will destroy the company or supercharge it.

The bull case sounds elegant. Uber trades "dirty" 30% human take rates for "clean" 20% AV take rates. Lower headline, higher margins. Insurance and driver incentives disappear. Uber becomes the aggregator sitting on top of a fragmented AV ecosystem, extracting rent from Tesla, Alphabet's Waymo, and everyone else.

One problem. The thesis depends almost entirely on that fragmentation actually happening.

## The unit economics

Uber's 30% take rate is inflated by pass-throughs: insurance and incentives that show up as revenue but get paid out immediately. Strip those away and contribution per ride is thin. With AVs, the operator carries the insurance and there are no driver bonuses. A "lower" 20% take rate might be more profitable per trip.

Here's an illustrative example. The numbers are rounded to show the shape of it, not taken from Uber's filings:

| | Fare | Uber revenue | Costs | Contribution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Human driver | $30 | $9 | ~$5 | ~$4 |
| Autonomous | $30 | $6 | ~$1 | ~$5 |

Lower revenue, higher contribution. The math works if Uber keeps that 20%.

## The steelman

Even if AV supply consolidates, Uber might hold its value. Consumers pick whichever app has the faster ETA or the lower price. Uber bundles mobility with Eats and a membership. And Uber provides real value: demand smoothing, payments, fraud prevention, dispatch optimisation.

Having an app is easy. Owning the habit is hard. And Uber currently owns that habit. But that's as far as the bull case goes.

## Where it gets shaky

The steelman assumes AV operators will always pay 20% for Uber's demand. But that math changes at scale.

Tesla has explicitly said it will run its own robotaxi network. Waymo already has its own consumer app, and a popular one. Most riders in San Francisco and Phoenix use the Waymo app directly. These aren't startups desperate for distribution. They're backed by mega-cap balance sheets.

Morgan Stanley recently published a model that has Uber capturing [about 22% of US autonomous ride-share trips by 2032](https://sherwood.news/tech/where-morgan-stanley-thinks-autonomous-taxis-will-be-in-2032/), with Waymo and Tesla taking most of the rest. That sounds pretty terrible for Uber. And it is still, if you ask me, the optimistic scenario.

## The fragmentation gamble

The key is this. If ten AV companies compete for Uber's demand, Uber holds the leverage. If two dominate and go direct, Uber is in trouble.

So the question investors need to ask is: will this market evolve toward fragmentation or toward consolidation?

In my opinion, the structural forces point toward consolidation. Running an AV fleet takes billions in R&D, it comes with huge data network effects, and it's constrained by city-by-city regulatory moats. That sounds to me like a market whose natural tendency is to consolidate into a handful of operators.

What Uber is doing about it is actually pretty clever. It's putting a lot of money into small AV players. It's backing Amazon's Zoox, partnering with WeRide, and funding a long list of small AV projects. All of it clearly meant to keep the market fragmented.

But that's not the whole story.

## Fare deflation

There's another dimension: what happens to the fare itself?

If AVs slash the cost per mile, competition pushes fares down. Uber could keep its 20% and still earn fewer dollars if fares compress from $30 to $18.

The question isn't just "20% or 10%?" It's "20% of what?"

## The bottom line

Two variables matter: how fast AVs arrive, and how the supply side is structured.

| | Fragmented supply | Consolidated supply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Slow AV rollout** | Uber wins | Uber is okay, a cash cow |
| **Fast AV rollout** | Uber wins big | Uber gets squeezed |

The honest bull case isn't "AV economics favour Uber." It's "AV timelines are slower than feared, and I'll take the cash flows while the dust settles."

That's defensible. Autonomy has been "two years away" for a decade. But betting that Uber becomes *the* aggregator of a fragmented AV world means believing Uber can manufacture market structure through capital deployment.

The structural forces point the other way. So Uber's skill at deploying capital matters more now than ever.

If you own Uber, know what you own: a bet on delayed AV timelines, not on favourable AV economics.

## Looking back from late 2026

Nothing since has changed the shape of the argument. Waymo kept expanding through 2026, [adding coverage in several US cities](https://electrek.co/2026/10/27/waymo-expansion-250-square-miles-five-cities/) and [announcing Toronto](https://waymo.com/blog/2026/10/waymo-in-toronto/), and Tesla kept launching its own robotaxi service city by city. Waymo still runs on Uber in some markets, but both big operators are building direct relationships with riders. That's the consolidated column of the table.
