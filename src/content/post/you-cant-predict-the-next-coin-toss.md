Between 2001 and 2011, Duke University surveyed US chief financial officers every quarter and asked them a simple question: what will the S&P 500 return over the next year? Then the researchers compared the answers with what the market actually did. [The forecasts were close to useless](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16215/w16215.pdf). The real return landed inside the CFOs' own 80% confidence range only about 36% of the time.

Some of the most powerful people in the financial world can't predict market movements. They have no clue what the market will do a year from now.

That's not because they're incompetent. They clearly aren't. The reason is much deeper.

If the big fish can't predict the market, then who can? How are we even thinking about investing?

Jeff Bezos famously said that when Amazon reports its results, they reflect decisions taken years earlier. That's how long it takes for results to show up. Big company directors don't care about monthly price movements. Not because they're careless, but because those numbers are meaningless.

But hang on. Performance is measured in price movements, right? How can they be meaningless?

## Tossing coins

Let's imagine a nice, shiny coin that I'm about to toss. Heads or tails?

If the coin is fair, it's 50-50. Obviously.

But what if the coin has come up heads three times in a row? Are heads or tails more likely now? Surprisingly for some, it's still 50% each. Nothing has changed.

The disappointing reality is that you can't predict the next toss. There's no moving average of the last seven throws, and no technical analysis that finds patterns in the rate of heads.

What you *can* estimate is the long-term result. You don't know whether the next toss will be heads or tails. But you know that over many tosses, the proportion of heads will be around 50%.

Believe it or not, that's a lot of information. If I offered you a bet that pays off if the proportion of heads after 100 tosses lands between 40% and 60%, you should take it. That happens about 96% of the time.

So you take the bet, and the first three tosses all come up tails. Should you get nervous? Was it a bad bet?

You know better than that. Three tosses aren't enough information to give up. Over the long run, you'll be proved right.

## What if the coin isn't fair?

Then things change. Before placing your bet, you're allowed to inspect the coin. You can balance it, weigh it, toss it yourself, even consult a coin expert. You can do an in-depth analysis and work out the real probabilities. Let's say you've done a good job, you're confident in it, and you estimate 30% heads and 70% tails.

What should you do? Easy. Bet on a high proportion of tails.

You do the analysis. You place the bet. The coin is tossed and the first three results are all heads. What now? Were you wrong? Should you trigger the stop loss and go home?

You know better than that too. If your estimate is right, the long run will prove it.

This is what an investment thesis is. The analysis is your inspection of the coin. The price moves are the tosses.

Nobody can predict the next toss. We can only estimate the trend, and that estimate will only be proved right (or wrong) after a lot of tosses. Not one, not three, not even six or twelve.

Checking every day whether the latest toss went your way doesn't help you. Praying for every new toss to confirm your analysis is not "keeping track of my investments". It only clouds your mind and gets in the way of your next analysis.

**Because the only thing you should care about is the quality of your analysis. Not the coin tosses.**

The only way to succeed in investing is to do an analysis good enough that you can tune out short-term fluctuations without losing any sleep over them.

There are plenty of snake-oil vendors out there trying to convince you the next toss can be predicted if only you follow them. You know better. Either they don't understand statistics, or they're trying to scam you.

Short-term movements are mathematically indistinguishable from random. It's only when those variations add up over time that you can start making estimates.

## Price movements are just noise

The world is bursting with information. Real and fake. Useful and useless. Investing is hard mainly because the most important part of the job is separating signal from noise. Most of the work is filtering out the crap so you can keep what's real and useful.

Most people would agree with that. But here's the pill that's hard to swallow: price movements are noise, not signal. They are not useful information.

Long-term price trends are a consequence of the nature of businesses. Short-term price swings tell you absolutely nothing about the quality of those businesses. They're fuelled by rumours, speculation and echo chambers.

The fundamental truth is that price changes are CONSEQUENCES, not CAUSES. If a business creates value and grows its profits, its value increases and its price *tends* to increase. Note the word "tends". Prices fluctuate a lot, but they follow a trend. The trend is shaped by the value of the company. The short-term fluctuations are indistinguishable from random.

I wrote that in October 2021, in the middle of a downturn. Everything was down: the S&P 500, the Nasdaq, gold, even "risk-free" Treasuries. Suddenly everyone was wondering whether the decisions they'd made a few weeks earlier were mistakes. I think that approach is deeply wrong. A month of falling prices is not evidence about how good your investments are. It's not a signal to review your thesis. It's a few more coin tosses.

It's noise, and noise should be ignored.
