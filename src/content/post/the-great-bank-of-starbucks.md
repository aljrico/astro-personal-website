Among fast-food chains, Starbucks is unique in how much it dominates mainstream culture. Adored or mocked, it's known everywhere in the world. Not as another coffee shop, but as *the* coffee shop.

Studying its financials, I noticed something interesting. From a purely financial point of view, Starbucks isn't only in the coffee business. Starbucks is a bank. It might even be your bank, without you knowing it.

## Origins

Starbucks started as a humble coffee shop. One of the first genius ideas in its history was to craft "the Starbucks experience". It wasn't only about the coffee. It was about the vibe that came with it.

In the 90s and early 2000s, public gathering spaces were in decline. Fewer communal places were being built, and many sat empty and forgotten. At the same time, more people were becoming self-employed or working from home. That created demand for friendly places with good WiFi, comfy chairs and a huge supply of caffeine. You may already see where this is going.

Starbucks was designed to be a third place in our lives, somewhere between the rigid formality of the office and the unfocused comfort of home. People looking for something in between found the perfect sweet spot.

Starbucks encouraged staff to be friendly and get to know the regulars. Make small talk. Create the vibe of a small-town neighbourhood. They even wrote each customer's name on their cup.

And Starbucks exploded, helped by great branding and consistent quality. It was never the best coffee you could find, but it was consistently, reliably good enough. It also avoided traditional franchising: the company believed tight control over details was crucial to the brand. Just look at its store count over time:

| Year | Stores |
| --- | --- |
| 1990 | 84 |
| 1995 | 677 |
| 2000 | ~3,500 |
| 2008 | ~16,700 |

Then it stalled. The global recession, competition from McDonald's and growing customer dissatisfaction took their toll, and in 2008 and 2009 Starbucks closed hundreds of stores.

## Embracing technology

After Howard Schultz returned as CEO in 2008, Starbucks started making a myriad of small changes everywhere, from better barista training to bringing technology into every store. One of the lowest-profile changes turned out to be the biggest: leaning hard into the Starbucks Card, and later the app, with loyalty rewards and mobile payments.

Instead of paying with cash or a credit card, you load money onto your Starbucks Card or app, then use it to buy coffee. In return you get discounts and the occasional free drink. That doesn't sound ground-breaking. I used to have something similar at the library for the printer.

What makes this one special is how massive Starbucks is. It's everywhere. Customers don't mind leaving sizeable amounts of money sitting on their cards, because they're confident they'll use it eventually. So much so that, by 2021, more than 40% of US purchases were paid through Starbucks' own card and app.

Add all of that up and you get to a shocking number: Starbucks has around $1.5 billion of customers' money sitting on its balance sheet. That's more than most individual US banks hold in deposits.

You could argue that's not Starbucks' money. Fair. It's the customers', and they'll claim it eventually. But in the meantime, Starbucks has effectively got a $1.5 billion loan for free. Zero interest. No cost.

And it gets better. Some people forget about that money and never use it. Some estimates put that at around 10%. That's a free gift from customers for Starbucks to use however it likes.

## A bank without the rules

But Starbucks isn't legally a bank. In most places you can't withdraw your Starbucks balance as cash. You can only spend it on Starbucks. If you have $50 on your card, there's generally no way to get those $50 back. You can only turn them into coffee.

So Starbucks doesn't have to hold that money under the regulations a bank faces. It doesn't need to keep reserves on hand. It can't suffer a run on deposits. Within its normal corporate rules, it can put that money to work however it wants, as long as it keeps giving you coffee.

Blimey. Starbucks could perfectly well build a mobile payments system, or even its own currency.

That's a scary thought for old-school banks like Bank of America. It's essentially a lightly regulated bank. Starbucks could even compete with PayPal or Square if it got serious about it.

It even has the perfect name for the currency: Starbucks.
