In 2018 I wrote [The Value of Bitcoin](/posts/the-value-of-bitcoin/), where I argued that Bitcoin struggles to work as a currency: too volatile to store value, and nobody uses it as a unit of account. Three years later I was looking at it from a different angle. What changed my mind was watching the network survive 2021. It made me ask a different question: not whether Bitcoin works as a currency, but what ties it to the real world at all.

## Moving half the servers in a week

Imagine if Amazon, Microsoft or Google were told, with a week's notice, that they had to move half of their worldwide server capacity to another country.

These companies handle a gazillion requests every day, and we've seen what happens when one of them goes down for even a day. How much downtime do you think we'd see? A week? A month? My guess is we'd keep seeing issues for at least a year, until they rebuilt and migrated all that processing power somewhere else.

That's what the Bitcoin network faced in 2021. Bitcoin is run by miners. Anyone can set up a machine to mine bitcoins and contribute to the network, so its "processing cluster" could be spread all over the world. But it wasn't. For whatever reason, most miners happened to be in China. Until the Chinese government decided to stop that. In the spring of 2021 it banned crypto mining, and around half of the network's computing power went offline.

How much downtime did the network experience? None. The hashrate roughly halved and blocks came more slowly for a few weeks, until the difficulty adjusted and miners set up again elsewhere. But the network never stopped. Transactions kept going through.

Because proof of work is so redundant, the network's stability doesn't depend even on its largest cluster. It really is that robust.

## Work as the arbiter of truth

Millions of machines around the globe are burning electricity on cryptographic shenanigans, trying to solve the puzzle left by the previous block. That might sound like a waste of energy, and it kind of is. But it's also what makes the whole thing work like magic. Work becomes, in a sense, the arbiter of truth. There's no central authority deciding what counts as a valid transaction. Truth is whatever is in the longest valid chain. Which is, by definition, the one with the most work put into it.

The more energy the network uses, the more secure its transactions become. Smaller, independent copycats are much more vulnerable. None of Bitcoin's forks comes anywhere close to its computing power. A tiny fraction of Bitcoin's miners could overwhelm any of them overnight. That's network effects at work.

You can copy Bitcoin. The code is open source. Go ahead and run your own. What's harder to copy is the horde of users and miners staking computing power on the network. You can't copy tens of thousands of nodes ensuring consensus. You can't replicate a massive, ever-growing community of developers pushing improvements. Lightning, Bitcoin's second layer, took years to build and roll out. Every new developer and user leaves the competition further behind.

It's like copying Wikipedia onto your own website. Sure, you're now hosting all the information. Now what? Are you going to convince the army of editors to work on your version instead of the original? Good freaking luck. Not to mention all the links pointing to Wikipedia instead of you. How do you replicate that? You just can't.

## A currency grounded in reality

Throughout history, currencies have tied their value to something tangible, scarce and limited in supply. Ideally something useful, too. Humanity has used cows, seashells, wheat, even cigarettes and noodle packs. Gold has been the most famous answer. It can be mined if you spend enough energy, it's scarce and it's tangible. But it can also be lost, it's massively expensive to move around, and we don't really use it for much.

Many people worry that Bitcoin "isn't real" or is "only digital". They think there's no real-world value tied to it. I think they're wrong. Arguably, Bitcoin is more tied to the real world than gold. What ties Bitcoin to the real world? **The answer is easy: energy.**

Power is needed to create, modify and validate every block. Power is what a miner spends to earn a bitcoin.

Adam Smith saw labour as the real measure of value, and money as a way to store and exchange it. He meant human labour, of course. Bitcoin is arguably the most literal version of that idea, requiring proof of work to mint every single new coin.
