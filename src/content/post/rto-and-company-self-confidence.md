Knowledge work is extremely hard to quantify and measure, even though it's the most valuable, moat-building kind of work a company can produce. When I study companies, I look for cultures that encourage and celebrate people who produce intangible, hard-to-measure but valuable results. That's what Google was famous for in the old days. It's what set Meta apart for a while.

Encouraging intangible work is obviously much easier said than done. Let me be honest for a moment: the vast majority of middle managers couldn't set genuinely SMART goals if their lives depended on it, and few companies have processes that make it easier. How companies work internally is often hard to see from the outside, beyond leaks and rumours. So it's very hard to tell which companies are good at measuring the productivity or impact of knowledge work.

But it's actually very easy to spot the companies that are terrible at it. Just look for this behaviour: **they desperately push return-to-office policies.**

You can see it in headlines, official announcements and public comments from leadership. They despise remote work and accuse workers of "slacking off" when they're allowed to work on their own terms. They think everyone will be more productive if they all sit in the same office at the same time. You can tell they think of the office as a factory.

Whenever I see that, I see a company with a bad working culture, a poor hiring strategy and no real understanding of how to measure knowledge work.

## Telling teams apart

Some teams benefit greatly from working in person. Others don't, really. Telling them apart is key. Companies that send everyone back to the office are companies that can't tell those teams apart.

My bet is they aren't even using the in-person time for tighter collaboration and a creative flow of ideas. They're just running an outdated template of control and oversight that distrusts individual contributors. That kind of culture destroys creativity, empowers bullies and antagonises employees. It's a huge red flag.

If employees slack off the moment their manager looks away, the company has deeper problems that harsher surveillance and expensive offices won't solve. It needs to rethink how it hires and promotes people, how it motivates and pays them, and how it operates. A company that distrusts and antagonises its people will only get workers who do the bare minimum to avoid getting fired. Which feeds management's paranoia about slacking. It's a vicious cycle and a hostile environment, and it won't produce great results. Especially in industries where knowledge and creative output are what matter.

## The same with AI

I see the same thing with AI. You can tell whether a company reacts to a breakthrough with "nice, we'll be more productive" or with "finally, we can get rid of all these leeches". The technology is the same. The mood is completely different. And so is that company's future.

A company's culture is its most important asset.
