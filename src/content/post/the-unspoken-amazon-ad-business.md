Everybody knows Google and Facebook are the giants of advertising. They dominate ads on Instagram, YouTube and Facebook.

There's a hot debate about whether one will dethrone the other (my bet is on Google, by the way). But we rarely hear about Amazon in this race. Is it insignificant?

It used to be. But the numbers are starting to creep up. Amazon now has around 12% of the US digital ad market. That might not sound like much, but for context: Amazon's ad business brings in more revenue than all of Twitter.

## Shoppers in the perfect mindset

Millions of people visit Amazon every day to buy things. That's a massive number of customers who are already thinking about buying something. They're not casually browsing and getting bombarded with products. They're actively looking for something to buy. And Amazon can "recommend" what to buy better than anyone.

When you're on YouTube, you just want to watch videos. You try to ignore most of the ads that come your way. Eventually one will catch you and make you want to buy something, but what are the chances, really? How many fail along the way?

When you're on Amazon, you have a concrete purpose. You want to buy something, or you're looking for something to buy. Amazon's recommendations are far more powerful because they hit you in exactly the right mindset.

Brands know this. That's why they pay a lot to show up at the top of Amazon's search results. In the US, Amazon is already the third-biggest ad business, right after Google and Facebook.

So far so good, but it's still the number three. It'll never beat Google or Facebook, right? Think again.

## The end of tracking

The world is changing. I can feel it in the water. Privacy is becoming a major concern for users, and regulation will eventually follow. That's a huge risk for many ad-based companies.

These companies don't only model your behaviour from what you willingly share in their apps. They also collect information about what you do elsewhere on your computer or phone. And that last part has its days numbered, in my opinion. Apple was the first to pull the plug on those permissions, and it wouldn't shock me if regulators ended up banning it too.

If you're one of these companies, the situation is this: you have to generate useful data yourself, from how people use your own products. You can't just take it from what they do elsewhere. That's kind of stalking them, and it's now frowned upon.

Google generates a great deal of data from its many services: Gmail, Maps, Search and so on.

Amazon already knows everything it needs to know about you the moment you land on the site. You want to buy stuff. What stuff? Whatever you type into the search bar. It couldn't be more direct.

And Facebook? Behaviour on Instagram or Facebook isn't that marketable. WhatsApp uses end-to-end encryption. Whatever your cousin tells you, they can't read your messages, and no, they can't listen to your voice notes either. Its entire business model relies on being able to follow its users outside its own apps and services.

Zuckerberg knows this. That's why Facebook is pivoting so hard into the metaverse. Creating a new playing field lets it build a new environment for new business. It's a bold move, and I respect that. I'm not buying just yet, though.

But whatever the metaverse ends up being, we can already see that the two giants fighting over online advertising won't be Google and Facebook. They'll be Google and Amazon.

Jeff Bezos once said, "Advertising is the price you pay for having an unremarkable product."

## Looking back

Half of this aged very well. Amazon's ad business kept growing fast and passed $56 billion in revenue in 2024. The other half didn't. Meta rebuilt its ad targeting around its own data and AI after Apple's tracking changes, and its ad business grew much faster than Amazon's in absolute terms. It turned out to be a three-way race, not a handover.
