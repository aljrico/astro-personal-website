You've probably never thought about this company, but you should. It might be the company that decides the future of silicon-based technology for the next ten or twenty years.

## How chips are made

**Design.** Companies like Nvidia and AMD design chips. These designs are blueprints that define how a chip should be made. That sounds simple, but it's not. Think of making chips as constructing a building. Architects don't lay bricks or paint walls, but their output defines the functionality, stability, style and quality of the building. The same is true for chip designers. The circuit blueprint defines the chip's capacity, performance, power consumption, and every electrical property you can think of.

Then there are the so-called Electronic Design Automation tools: highly specialised software that chip designers use to draw those blueprints. Cadence and Synopsys provide the best and most widely used ones.

**Foundries.** Companies like Intel and TSMC run massive factories that take those blueprints and turn them into physical chips. They do it by engraving the design onto silicon wafers. Imagine a disc that's completely plain and smooth. The foundry then uses enormous machines to tattoo the chip blueprint onto it, leaving the wafer looking like Michael Scofield's back.

These discs aren't metal. They're extremely pure silicon crystals, above 99.9999999% pure, and billions are being spent to push that further. Then, after obsessively purifying the wafers to ridiculous decimal places, companies like Applied Materials and Axcelis use "ion implantation" machines to add some impurities back, on purpose, to create the electrical properties a transistor needs. Lam Research and Applied Materials also make the deposition tools that coat and clean the wafers along the way.

And the machines that do the tattooing aren't really lasers. The most advanced ones are Extreme Ultraviolet lithography machines (EUV): absolutely massive systems with a series of mirrors that bounce and focus ultraviolet light with extreme precision. The finer the line you can draw, the more intricate the designs you can cram onto a wafer, and the crazier chip designers can get. That's why you constantly hear about the nanometre scale of chips. But making those lines even finer is extremely hard, so the machines keep getting bigger and more complex.

There aren't many companies in the world with the know-how to build lithography machines. And at the leading edge, there's honestly only one. That is ASML.

## A short detour: the 2021 chip shortage

When COVID hit, carmakers and electronics companies expected sales to collapse, so they cut their chip orders. Chipmakers shut down production lines in response. Then demand for laptops, screens and headphones exploded as everyone started working from home. Restarting a fab is slow and expensive, so the world ran into one of its biggest chip shortages ever. Which raised the obvious question: why are chip factories so hard to scale? A big part of the answer is the machines inside them.

## How ASML got here

Competition between chipmakers to stay at the top is fierce. Falling behind even slightly can mean total disaster. Just look at how quickly Intel got dethroned. A curious consequence is that almost all leading chipmakers use the same machines. If one machine is the best at producing chips, you have to use it. Any other option sets you up for failure.

ASML was founded in 1984 as a joint venture between the Dutch conglomerate Philips and ASM International (Advanced Semiconductor Materials International). The idea was to use something like a slide projector to imprint chip designs onto silicon.

The beginnings were rough. Nikon and Canon machines were faster and more powerful. They were simply better. ASM International pulled out within a few years, and Philips eventually took the company public in 1995 and sold down its stake. But ASML didn't give up. Through the 1990s it clawed its way up against the Japanese giants, and by the early 2000s it had become the market leader.

How did ASML stand out from the pack? It invested heavily to keep up with Moore's Law. In the mid-2000s it bet on immersion lithography, putting a thin layer of water between the lens and the wafer to sharpen the image. Its TWINSCAN immersion machines were a huge success.

But it didn't stop there. ASML also poured money into Extreme Ultraviolet lithography. You can think of EUV as very energetic light aimed at very specific spots on the silicon.

The problem is that EUV light gets absorbed by pretty much all matter, including air and glass. So the machine needs a vacuum, special photomasks, multi-layered reflectors and a complex system of mirrors instead of lenses. It's extremely complex. It's expensive. It's hard to manufacture, hard to operate and almost impossible to transport. Whoever uses it has to redesign their fabs around it.

Rivals eventually decided EUV was too expensive and too hard. ASML kept going, spending billions over two decades. And the bet was so important to the whole industry that in 2012 Intel, TSMC and Samsung, all competitors, together bought 23% of ASML and co-funded its research. That gave ASML the capital to get EUV into production.

ASML shipped its first EUV system built for volume manufacturing, the NXE:3400B, in 2017, and chipmakers started mass-producing chips with EUV in 2019. Orders piled up. But building these machines is a gargantuan task. ASML has to coordinate thousands of suppliers around the world, and some parts, like the Zeiss optics, take many months to make. Shipping them is even crazier. ASML's own figures for a single machine:

- It travels in 40 shipping containers.
- It needs 20 trucks and three Boeing 747s to get to Asia.
- The assembled machine weighs around 180 tons.

It's no surprise that NO ONE ELSE builds these beasts. ASML has around 90% of the lithography market, and 100% of EUV.

And the market keeps growing. Chips are everywhere: data centres, cars, laptops, even Bitcoin mining. The world needs not just more of them, but faster and more efficient ones.

There's one more piece. What happens if one of these machines hiccups for a couple of days? Someone has to maintain them, and they're so complicated that the obvious experts are ASML's own engineers. Each EUV machine costs well over $100M, and servicing and upgrading the installed base is a large, recurring business on top of that.

## The geopolitics of chips (November 2022)

Intel was historically unbeatable, but it has been bleeding market share to AMD and Nvidia. Intel's recent chips don't hold a candle to AMD's in performance per watt, and performance per watt is the top priority for pretty much everyone. It's very telling that some of the best news Apple has given investors in the last five years is its plan to drop Intel chips completely.

Why can AMD, Nvidia and even Apple make better chips than Intel? The answer is the same for all of them. They rely on the same manufacturer: TSMC, which has taken the semiconductor world by storm. And at the centre of TSMC's incredibly complicated operation sits the Dutch company I can't stop talking about. ASML's machines let chip designers cram more complicated circuits into smaller chips. That's a big reason AMD has been able to go crazy with its designs and humiliate Intel.

Intel is realising this. Its plan for a comeback is essentially to become the next TSMC, and to do that it has struck a deal with ASML to get the same fancy machinery.

Make no mistake. Nobody in the world is even close to being able to produce what ASML produces.

And that is a big geopolitical problem. The world runs on chips. What was true of oil in the last century is true of chips now. We can burn oil to produce energy. We can spin turbines with wind. Hell, we've even learned to bombard atoms with neutrons until they split, and use the heat to boil water and spin more turbines. If countries clash, everyone can get energy one way or another. **The real bottleneck in the case of open conflict is processing power**: the smart machines that turn all that energy into something useful.

Big data, cloud computing, AI, blockchain. Whatever you're excited about, it runs on computing power. That means a huge number of high-performance chips, almost all of them manufactured in TSMC's factories in Taiwan. This is why Taiwan is a geopolitical ticking time bomb.

Governments know this. Both Europe and the US have announced plans to throw buckets of cash at whoever helps build chip manufacturing at home. That might be Intel's biggest selling point right now, since it ticks every box for that public funding. And the most crucial step in Intel's plan to build fabs in Europe and the US? Buying ASML machines.

It all converges on the same company. AMD overtaking Intel. Nvidia's rise. Apple's M1 shock to the laptop market. TSMC's supremacy. All of it is tied to ASML's machines.

That could make ASML look like a no-brainer. And it almost is. But with great power comes great responsibility. Sitting at the centre of a global geopolitical storm is not a comfortable spot. For security reasons, governments will try to meddle with the business. Maybe by capping what ASML can export to this or that country. Maybe by forcing lower prices on certain buyers. Maybe by passing laws that limit what ASML can and can't do.

That might be ASML's biggest risk.

## Postscript (November 2026)

That's roughly what happened. ASML had never been allowed to ship EUV machines to China, and from 2023 the Dutch government, under pressure from the US, also began requiring export licences for ASML's most advanced immersion machines going there, then tightened the rules again in the following years.
