Let's start by stating the obvious. Apple is a beast. At this point you can think of it as a money-printing machine. Its free cash flow was $57 billion in 2019 and $71 billion in 2020, and in 2021 it will churn out at least $80 billion, even with pessimistic estimates.

That's insane. An obscene amount of money.

Apple makes the most profitable product in so many categories that each one could be its own company, and each would rank near the top of the S&P 500 by revenue. It sells the world's most profitable laptop, desktop, tablet, smartwatch, wireless headphones and, of course, smartphone.

Many people argue Steve Jobs was the only true genius at Apple. Some even say Tim Cook has been running the company on autopilot since Steve left us.

I categorically disagree. I think Tim Cook is one of the most underrated CEOs out there. He really is a genius, just a different sort. The kind that doesn't dominate the headlines. Less flashy, but no less influential or important.

## Did Apple stop innovating?

When Steve Ballmer took over Microsoft, the company got stuck milking its existing products and burning value year after year. Its stock went nowhere while the rest of the industry soared. With almost no focus on innovation, Microsoft lived off established products like Windows and Office. It missed smartphones, missed the head start in cloud, and missed the rise of social.

Some people argue Apple has been living through a similar era under Tim Cook. The legendary Steve Jobs gave us the Mac, the iPod, the iPhone and the iPad, and completely revolutionised the market. Cook, the story goes, is a supply chain genius but not the product mastermind Jobs was, and it shows.

There's some truth to that. But under Tim Cook, Apple's stock price has gone up about tenfold over the last ten years, making it the most valuable company on this good planet. Microsoft's stock was flat for Ballmer's entire reign.

How? Because Cook, while not the product genius Jobs was, is extremely valuable in his own way. He has combined finely tuned operations with a good amount of innovation. Maybe not as revolutionary as Jobs', but extremely good nonetheless. Under Cook, Apple launched the Apple Watch, the impressive iPad Pro and the underrated AirPods. It also kicked Intel out and released the M1.

People tend to think the sooner you launch a revolutionary product, the better. It's not that simple. Take Google Glass. It was cool, useful and very revolutionary. It was also a massive failure. Why? The world wasn't ready. Not just people's minds, but the ecosystem, the developers and the products around it. Cook keeps stressing the importance of augmented reality, and I think Apple is laying the groundwork to launch into it when the time is right, and not a minute earlier.

## The real change: Apple's product strategy

The market has noticed. Apple's market cap has grown by more than $1.5 trillion since 2018. What happened in 2018? A subtle but crucial change in how Apple organises its products. That was Tim Cook's genius.

### The directed product line

For years, Apple built what I call a "directed product line". That's a polite way of saying it laser-focused on a small set of hugely sought-after products, and used the rest of the catalogue as an upsell.

Remember the monitor stand Apple sold for $999? That's my favourite example. Nobody in their right mind would buy it on its own. But if you own an Apple display, it's probably the only perfectly matching stand you'll find. So if you really need a perfectly matching stand, Apple more or less forces you to buy it.

For years, this was Apple's strategy across the whole catalogue. To make it work, Apple created what I call "gateway" products. A gateway product is so far ahead of the competition, and so exclusive, that everyone wants it. But once you buy it, you're in. The rest of the catalogue is great mostly at syncing with the gateway product, and the gateway product only really syncs with the rest of the ecosystem.

**The objective is to hook new customers through the gateway product, to get them in. And once they're in, they end up buying everything else.**

You can see where this is going. The first Apple product anyone bought was an iPhone. New customers came almost exclusively through the iPhone. Everything else was basically upselling.

The Apple Watch is another example. Say you had a Google Pixel and wanted a smartwatch. The Apple Watch simply wasn't an option. You couldn't pair the two, and you'd lose most of the Watch's features. That was very much on purpose. If you wanted an Apple Watch, you had to buy an iPhone too.

In the same way, the iPad and the MacBook partly relied on the success of the iPhone. If the iPhone fell behind, so did the iPad. The strategy built a clear chain that pushed users through Apple's product line:

Wearable → iPhone → iPad → MacBook → Mac Pro

It seems like a genius move until you realise this: the whole company relies on the success of one single product. Only one. If the iPhone flops, the whole company goes off a cliff.

That's way too much risk for a company as big as Apple. And it was the main reason I hesitated to invest in it for years. Fears of exactly this came up when iPhone sales started slowing around 2018. The company had an exposed Achilles' heel, and everyone was looking at it.

### The undirected product line

That changed around 2018, with Tim Cook pushing Apple away from such a directed product line.

The idea now is that every Apple product should be a gateway on its own. Does that mean opening up the famous walled garden? Not at all. Every Apple product is still worth more if you own other Apple products. The difference is that each product is now meant to bring new customers into the ecosystem by itself.

Apple does this by giving each product a defined role in the ecosystem. For example:

- iPads and Macs are clearly content creation tools.
- iPhones are the main camera and video player.
- Wearables handle specific workflows that used to be handled by iPhones or iPads.

The exciting part is that Apple is securing its future by taking pressure off the iPhone. Since each category handles specific workflows, part of Apple's focus is on creating entirely new workflows that those devices handle better.

Plenty of companies, Samsung and Microsoft among them, have copied the old Apple playbook and tried to funnel users through a gateway product. Apple has already moved on. I think it has changed in the right direction, and it's a safer investment than ever.

## The proof: the M1 is a stealth revolution

I've been saying it since it was announced, and people are starting to realise it now: Apple's M1 Macs are truly revolutionary products. From the impressive performance to the outstanding battery life, this generation is a substantial step up in a laptop market that had been pretty stale for years.

Here's why I call the M1 MacBooks an experiment rather than the real revolution. An outlandishly successful experiment, the kind Google and Microsoft dream of. The MacBook line-up didn't change. Same chassis, same battery, same fans, same everything. The M1 was simply stuffed into a machine designed to hold an Intel chip. Intel's chips run much hotter, which means big, expensive fans, and they're much more power-hungry, which means a big, heavy battery.

Using the old design worked surprisingly well, but it didn't unlock the M1's full potential. Partly because the machine was built for an Intel chip, and partly because it was a first-generation product. It wasn't nearly as powerful as AMD's flagships, which, by the way, are leaving Intel in the dust.

**I do believe Apple silicon will change everything.**

Processing efficiency is everything. Period. Raw power is useless if it cooks your thermals or drains your battery. Intel has long bragged about the "Turbo Boost" its chips switch on when needed. Do you know what happens when it runs too long? The chip gets so hot that it has to throttle itself down to avoid damage. It's clumsy and silly. A way to show off without delivering anything of substance.

It's like comparing Bruce Lee with the random shredded, croissant-shaped guy at your gym. The gym guy might have the looks, but you know Bruce would punch harder.

The M1 has no turbo mode. It doesn't need one. It can run at its maximum clock speed indefinitely, without pausing to cool off.

To be honest, we're not there yet. Despite the massive gap in efficiency, the M1 doesn't hold a candle to the absolute performance of some high-end x86 beasts, especially in graphics, which is indispensable for gaming. That's why the next generation matters so much. If Apple pulls it off, it could reshape what we all expect from a laptop. Apple might be about to change the world again.

Why "stealth"? Because Apple seems shy about its innovation. Many analysts insist Apple is just a consumer goods company that doesn't innovate anymore. No offence, but I think they're clueless. They're confusing different leadership styles. Tim Cook is nowhere near as flamboyant or charismatic as Steve was. He's more discreet and humble. He's the kind of guy who does something impressive without going around bragging about it, and some people are missing all the amazing stuff he's pulling off.

I expect this to speed up Apple's gains in the PC market, expanding and diversifying its revenue. For years I saw Apple as over-reliant on iPhone sales, and too exposed to any misstep in the phone market. That has changed. Apple has shown it can diversify, expand into new markets, and keep innovating.

## Looking back

When I wrote the M1 piece, everyone expected Apple's October 2021 event to bring the "M2". It didn't. It brought the M1 Pro and M1 Max, which were exactly the chips I was hoping for: Apple silicon in a MacBook Pro designed around it from scratch. The M2 came the following summer.

Four years later, the bet on Macs looked even better. In Apple's results for the last quarter of fiscal 2025, Mac sales were up 13% while the overall laptop market grew about 4%. And Apple had just crossed $4 trillion in market cap.
