Cleanup in the morning. Postcard queue was rebuilding maps twice on campaign tab switches — once from the isolate callback, once from paint if the result wasn't ready. Fixed. Opacity fades in place now. The campaign district got faster and it feels smoother.

Then the real thing landed: star-earned tower progression. Needle and Bloom free. Frost at 25 stars, Ember at 50, Lance at 100. The Field Guide got a second tab — TURRETS — with live arenas. Each tower in its own little battle, zoomed to a 2.6-cell window, locked cards showing how many stars away.

The Lance redesign was the moment. Used to be this generic pointy thing. Now it's dark glass, a rounded tile beneath. When it focuses — pulls power — the glass goes white-hot. Nine lilac motes accelerate in from 1.5 cells. Wrote a fragment shader for it, a geometric drain effect that washes the board in desaturation, heat shimmer cutting through. User asked to push it harder. Then asked me to dial every knob back. First instinct was right.

Tower toast came at the end. After you win, stars land on the end card, and a dark panel drops from the top: "FROST UNLOCKED" with a small haptic thud, or the next tower dimmed with the progress bar filling. It's got its own queue, survives the Continue button, dismisses on tap. The juice is gentle. It lands.

This is the stuff — visuals clicking into place, animation feels right, the screen does exactly what I pictured.

But before the evening's balance doom, the day pivoted toward something bigger.

Campaign UI was muddy. Three tiny cards with blurry circle lenses, a yellow Play tag on each, and most of the screen empty. User hated it. Redesigned everything from scratch.

Started by restyling the cards. Switched fonts while I was at it — bundled TeX Gyre Heros (GUST license, needed for Android since Helvetica won't ship). Wordmark got Chakra Petch Bold. Harder, more geometric. Techy/sci-fi feel. User kept it. Campaign cards got hand-picked pastel colors per region — lavender, coral, poppy, sky blue. Each region got a proper postcard lens showing the coast or the walls or the river, rimmed in that region's color. The old muddy aesthetic died fast.

Then came the moment where the direction split. Mocked two approaches offline — map cards vs a journey line. User picked the journey. A winding path down the screen, regions as stations alternating left and right, each a postcard lens onto a real level, ringed by 25 ticks showing clears and stars. The road fills as you progress.

The home page got torn apart too. Dropped the demo art, built a city grid in grey and white — two wide streets, one diagonal cutting across. Static streets, but hung invaders on a ticker walking three routes at game speed. Mad scientist mode: Invaders walk, camera pans over empty city, cards drop in staggered from above with easeOutBack. The whole thing has motion.

That pulled me toward the invaders themselves. They were bland swimmer outlines. Spent the evening redrawing the whole roster. Started with soft single-eyed cells, then went deeper. Motes became tadpoles with a kick-and-glide swim pattern. Swarms are water fleas now, hopping and drifting. Tanks are manta rays. Colonies burst into rosettes of six tiny darts instead of five motes. Tested everything via a debug switcher: rejected krill, dividing cells, spirillum. Settled on the seafaring theme — everything feels organic now, purposeful. Shipped the finalists. First TestFlight build with the new roster went out.

The infrastructure sprint happened in parallel. Built the earned-tower system properly — ProgressStore.arsenal, TurretKind carrying unlock costs and ordering. All four towers now require stars you actually earned by playing. Seed tool estimates: Frost around level 14, Ember level 26, Lance at level 51. Sweetspot — you unlock them while you're still learning what they do.

But the campaign wasn't just polished. It got rebuilt.

Realized the old region system was broken for long-term design — reordering would break save data, adding levels per region was a mess. Started fresh with a regional key system, each region with its own set of 25 levels. Rebuilt the campaign as four separate regions with their own aesthetics and difficulty curves.

Softwood came first. 25 authored levels with static cliffs scattered across the board. Simple. Then it got complicated.

Apricot Bay shaped itself into a bay. Wrote a new terrain type: Lake, with per-column first-water row. The generator creates bays via seeded sine headlands. Tried six different looks. Ziggurat, pointy peaks, too smooth, too rough. Landed on a seismograph-style coast — broad bends, detail masked by a slow patch envelope. Bay cells are 19% fewer buildable squares than Softwood, but the shape sings. Iterated colors live: started #FFD4B8 (too warm against white rivers), settled on #EED3B8 sampled from a Mini Motorways reference. Cliffs are terracotta — #D3805C, #E3A360, #F7CD80. User loved the headlands idea: one sea cliff per level, 1–2 land cliffs. Small 4–6-cell rocks scattered around, preferring over river where they tunnel under. Seven percent of dailies had no mesa on first pass — patched by trying smaller variants.

Moss Valley became a canyon. Two wall cliffs with a sine meander — sway 1–1.9 cells, period 7–12 rows, width breathing 3.9–6.5. Needed the contours to flow. Tried boxy bulges first (glitched at necks), switched to erosion via Chaikin rounding. The walls look carved now. Ground #CFE8D5, tiers #2F6B57, #3F7F68, #58977C — Mini Motorways references again. No free cliffs in Moss Valley. The canyon IS the difficulty. Bot was losing too many lives early on, so added a growth multiplier: bodies and HP scale with a climb factor per region. Dialed it until the curve looked right.

Then Rust Mesa. A boxy mesa used to sit over the first river and the user hated it. Grew it instead — organically, with Chaikin rounding to smooth the contours. Rivers tunnel under it, 3–8 cells hidden. New rivers can burrow too, but only once, keeping Rust Mesa's identity intact. Palette: ground #ECD9B0, cliffs #A1402D, #BF5A3E, #DB7D58. The mesa IS the difficulty, same call as Moss Valley.

Each region needed its own map seeds. Ran the picker 40 times per region, targeting a lives-lost curve: early levels near zero, scaling up to 5–6 late. Softwood and Apricot Bay came out smooth. Moss Valley was lumpy — 0–1 lives until level 10, then flat 3s mid-region, 6 late. Rust Mesa better. All four regions hit their targets eventually.

The daily challenge pulled from all four regions now — same region for everyone per day, seeded from the date. Gives players a different region to learn each morning.

Wove each region into the campaign's journey. The level selector became a winding road per region — 25 stops, seeded sine curves (Softwood gentle, Apricot Bay long sweeps, Moss Valley switchbacks). Each stop is a locked or unlocked level marker snapped to the road. Level 10 acts as the unlock gate for the next region, marked with a quiet sighting card: "Clear level 10 to unlock Apricot Bay." The road fills from 0 to 100% as you clear more levels, dashed in Play yellow up to your current region.

Built the level maps off the UI thread. That's 100 levels worth of session generation, and Moss Valley/Rust Mesa get expensive. Moved it to Isolate.run, queued the nearest-next-level first, dropped old scenes when leaving. Offscreen rendering helped iterate the layout — AnimatedSwitcher was weird (loose constraints on childless painted content), fixed with StackFit.expand. Screenshot bursts with `simctl` caught the arrival animations landing.

Then came the Field Guide turrets tab — each tower on a real board zoomed to 2.6 cells, matched prey circling, live unlocks counting up. Locked cards show how many stars away. User loved the Lens glass going white-hot while draining. Wrote the shader (Impeller fragment shader) for the drain effect — 75% desaturate, 22% dim, ~1.2 cells coverage, 1.2px heat shimmer. First cut was too aggressive. User asked to dial it back. I walked every knob back to the original values. First instinct was right.

Shipped six builds through TestFlight — each one carrying another piece. Invaders walked the home grid. Campaign cards restyled. Regions built. Palettes locked. Then the whole thing collapsed at the meta level.

Ran the greedy bot on all 100 campaign levels. It builds nothing but needles. Thirty-six needles. Forty needles. Wins every single level. Loses maybe four lives across the whole campaign. The unlock system is meaningless.

The problem isn't balance knobs. It's that needles are so efficient they're the only choice. Frost needs a niche. Lance needs a reason. Bloom can't be a filler tower. But that's not a tuning problem — that's a system problem. Greedy optimization finds the dominant strategy and stays there. Real play is different. A human trying all the towers will discover weaknesses. A bot that only measures "win the level" will never care.

Needed a smarter bot. One that tries different rosters, learns what each tower handles well. That's the evening's unfinished business.

The gap sits between the beautiful things I made today and the system that breaks it. The code works. The meta doesn't. But the invaders shipped. The regions shipped. The journey shipped. The towers shipped. The Lens shipped with a white-hot moment that lands.

Sometimes you build something that looks perfect and plays broken. You see both at the same time. The polish doesn't fix the foundation. Neither does the balance pass.

Tomorrow, probably, the balance.
