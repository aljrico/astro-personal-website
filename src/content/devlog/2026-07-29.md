Today's output, as of lunch: one icon. Total.

The Cases button on the home screen has been bugging me for weeks. It had this book icon that never quite said "cases." It said "library," maybe "diary." Close enough that nobody would complain, wrong enough that I noticed it every single time I opened the app. That kind of wrong is worse than broken. Broken you fix. Slightly-off you just live with, quietly annoyed, until one day you snap.

Today I snapped.

First stop: the icon packs I already own, the ones sitting in iCloud that I keep telling myself justify their price. Surely someone drew a folder. No. Envelopes, clipboards, scrolls — a whole stationery shop, no folders. Who makes an icon pack without a folder? Rejected the lot.

So I made my own. Pasted in a folder icon I liked and let the machine do the tracing — I pointed, it drew, duotone SVG out the other end. Then it retinted the thing from its boring navy and grey into the app's palette: the red chocolate and the mango shade, colors I named months ago and still stand behind. This is the agentic thing working exactly the way I want it to. I didn't touch a path point. I just kept saying "warmer" and "no, the other layer" and watched it happen. Then the fun part: three variants — single folder, pair, stack — swapping them live with hot reload like trying on jackets. Change a line, watch the screen, feel it. This is the part of the work I'd do for free. The stack won immediately. A stack of folders says *cases* — plural, accumulating, work waiting inside. The single folder just said "folder." The pair said nothing at all.

(Half of that icon work actually happened yesterday, apparently. I wouldn't know — yesterday's devlog notes died with the note-taking agent, mid-write, at the session limit. The machine that remembers my days forgot one. There's something almost poetic about that. The commits survived; the feelings didn't. Git remembers what, never why.)

Committed, pushed to the release branch. One icon, one commit. I genuinely thought that was the whole day.

It wasn't.

Because somewhere in the afternoon the folder stack got ambitious. The stats sheet has been sitting there half-empty for ages, and I've had this idea rattling around: show players how *fast* they're actually getting. Not a number. A curve. So I built a Performance section — a folder-tab panel, one tab per difficulty, the tabs generated straight off the difficulty enum so they can never drift out of sync, and behind each tab a density curve of your completion times per environment.

Actual density curves. Gaussian KDE over the times pulled from the XP ledger. And here's where the physicist in me — usually a liability, usually the guy making spreadsheets instead of shipping — finally got to earn his keep. The raw curves clipped ugly at zero, because of course they did, times can't be negative but the kernel doesn't know that. Reflected the mass at the zero boundary, extended the tail a couple of bandwidths past the 95th percentile so the curve fades out instead of getting guillotined, checked the area actually integrates to one. Twenty minutes of feeling like I still remember something from a past life. The curves came out *clean*. Three-pixel stroke, a fill at a third opacity, sitting on a white-washed inset tile. I kept opening the sheet just to look at them, the same way I kept triggering the folder icon this morning.

The colors fought back, though. Each environment needed its own representative color, and I fumbled it twice. First instinct: reuse palette colors I already had lying around — orient, watermelon, names that sound right and meant nothing here. Wrong. Second instinct: pick some tasteful pastels by hand. Also wrong — watered-down nothing on the cream background. Third try, the obvious one in hindsight: go to the source. Sampled the actual water sprite from the islands environment for its blue — cove water, I'm calling it, a proper #1cadf4 — sampled the pink trees from the grove for a blossom pink, #ff608a, and the cottage got the red chocolate it always secretly was. The environments already knew what color they were. I just had to ask them instead of inventing an answer. Then made it structural — a required `representativeColor` on every casebook, a proper registry, so no screen ever guesses an environment's color again. Two bad attempts and one decision I'll actually thank myself for.

Then the gating. You can't draw a density curve from two data points, so environments below a sample threshold show a progress bar instead — solve X more cases to unlock the chart. The threshold is a single constant, and I nudged it up and down twice during the afternoon before parking it at six. Some numbers you don't calculate, you feel out. The nice part is that one constant drives everything — the gate, the bars, even the copy, which is parameterized so the number rides inside the string. Nudge the constant and the whole sheet reshuffles itself, no re-translation, no chasing stale numbers through nine locales every time I change my mind. Past me set that up without knowing today's me would flip-flop four times. Thanks, past me.

The gating meant empty-state copy in nine languages, which is where the machine nearly embarrassed me. The English said "unlock the plot." Plot, as in chart. Sonnet came back with plot, as in *storyline*, in every single one of the eight other languages. La trama. Die Handlung. Eight locales promising players they'd unlock the narrative if they just solved more sudokus. Caught it because Spanish is mine and "la trama" jumped off the screen like a typo in your own name. Hostia. Fixed all eight — gráfica, Diagramm, graphique, the words that actually mean chart. A reminder that the machine is brilliant right up until it's confidently, fluently wrong — and the human in the loop is not decorative.

The whole afternoon ran on the orchestrator pattern I've been settling into: Opus builds the hard thing — the KDE, the tab panel — Sonnet handles copy and progress bars, and Fable does the surgical one-value edits while I point at the threshold and say "lower." And for anything visual, the loop is always the same: hot reload, look at it, judge it, say the next word. Conducting, not typing. It's fast in a way that still feels slightly illegal.

Committed, pushed, and I told myself that was it. 1.13.0, riding out with the icon and the curves.

It wasn't it. Again.

Because late afternoon, in a second session running parallel to the stats one, I went after something that's been on the wishlist forever: placement juice. Right now you tap a suspect in the tray and it just *appears* in the cell. Functional. Dead. What I wanted was the sprite to *fly* — leave the tray, arc through the air, land in the cell with a little swell. So that's what it does now. A quadratic bezier arc, 180 milliseconds — I started at 280 and it felt like watching someone carry a tray of drinks across a room; cut it and suddenly it's a flick, a dart, exactly right. Sine swell on landing so the sprite puffs up as it touches down.

And here's the detail I'm actually proud of: everything that used to fire on the tap — the ink ripple, the occupant reveal, the haptics — now fires on the *landing*. Light haptic impact when the sprite launches, medium when it lands. Tap, flight, *tac TAC*. A rising two-beat. You feel the piece leave your hand and arrive. I sat there placing suspects over and over like the folder icon this morning, like the curves this afternoon. Third toy of the day. Apparently that's just how I know something's done — I can't stop touching it.

The plumbing needed a real structure: a rect registry so tray and cells report where they are on screen, a choreographer that can run concurrent flights per sequence, and all the tuning constants parked at the top of one file so I could hot-reload numbers and feel them, the same loop as always. The tray icon got a proper animated dip instead of a bare opacity snap. Tutorial keeps the old instant path — its controller overrides placement wholesale and I'm not untangling that today — and if no anchors are registered, everything falls back to instant. Juice that degrades to function.

Then Opus earned its keep in a way I didn't ask for. Deferring the ripple to landing made forty tests fail, and instead of patching the tests it followed the smell down and found a *latent production bug* — the hand-spawned ink ripples were leaking tickers on screen teardown. Place a suspect and immediately back out of the screen: crash. That bug was already live. Shipped. Sitting in production waiting for a fast-fingered player. Now the ripples are tracked and disposed properly. I asked for an animation and got a crash fix I didn't know I needed. That's the second time today the machine went beyond the spec and was right to. Tests went from 650 to 656, analyze clean.

Even committing it was mildly ridiculous — the other session had its scratch notes dirty in the shared repo, so the commit went in through a temp-index commit-tree dance to avoid sweeping up someone else's mess. Two of me working the same repo at once, politely stepping around each other's laundry. This is apparently my life now.

And still the evening had more, because the thing about a chart you keep opening just to look at is that eventually you stop admiring it and start poking it. Three more commits on the stats, all pushed to the release branch, all the kind of work that only happens when a feature has already won you over.

First, a guard I'll never see fire: capped the curves to the most recent thousand solves per environment. Nobody has a thousand solves yet. But someone will, and a KDE over an unbounded ledger is a slow-motion problem I'd rather kill now, while the code is warm in my head, than rediscover in a profiler next year. And honestly the cap is better data anyway — your last thousand solves say who you *are*; the ones from two years ago say who you were. Recency as a feature, perf as the excuse. Or the other way around. Either way, one constant, and it's handled.

Then the legend grew hands. The chips under the chart were just labels this morning; now you tap them to hide and show each environment's curve, and the chart rescales itself to whatever's still visible. Hide the noisy environment and suddenly the other two get the whole canvas to stretch out in. It's the toy factor — I sat there toggling curves on and off like a kid with a light switch. The one bit of actual thinking: keeping *qualified* and *visible* as separate concepts. Whether an environment has enough data to earn a curve and whether you currently want to look at it are different questions, and the moment I tried to cram them into one flag the logic got weird. Split them, and everything downstream went obvious. Wrote three tests for the data layer to pin it down. For a guy who thinks code quality is overrated, the test count keeps creeping up. Turns out I don't test to be virtuous; I test the stuff I don't want to think about ever again.

And then the cap wasn't enough. A hard cutoff at a thousand solves is a cliff — solve number 1,000 counts fully, solve 1,001 vanishes. That's not how memory works. That's not how *skill* works. So the last stats commit of the night made it continuous: exponential recency weighting inside the KDE itself. Every solve gets a weight that halves every 150 solves back — half-life, one more constant with a physics name, because apparently today I'm allowed. Your recent games shape the curve; your ancient ones fade into the background instead of falling off a ledge. Opus went beyond the spec here too — weighted the Silverman bandwidth with an effective sample size, weighted the p95 for the domain, the whole thing done properly instead of just bolting weights onto the kernel and hoping. I asked for a feature and got the statistically honest version of it. Qualification stays on raw counts, though — you either have the data or you don't; how much I *trust* each point is a separate question. Same lesson as the legend, one layer deeper. Three more tests, including one that checks the curve's peak actually shifts when your recent times improve. If you've been getting faster, the chart should say so. Now it does.

The threshold went back up to ten while I was in there, riding along in the legend commit. Six felt generous this afternoon; with real curves in front of me, ten feels honest. A density estimate from six points is a horoscope. Fourth nudge today. Probably not the last.

So the morning ledger said one icon and the physicist grumbled about the hours. By evening the same release branch has reflection-corrected, recency-weighted, interactively-legended kernel densities in a sudoku app — objectively ridiculous, I love it — plus suspects that fly through the air and land with a *tac TAC*, plus one production crash that quietly died before anyone met it. Maybe five users will read those curves. Everyone will feel the flight. And it's the same tax logic as the icon either way: I'll see it. And now when I do, it'll be right.

Venga. Next.
