Today's output, as of lunch: one icon. Total.

The Cases button on the home screen has been bugging me for weeks. It had this book icon that never quite said "cases." It said "library," maybe "diary." Close enough that nobody would complain, wrong enough that I noticed it every single time I opened the app. That kind of wrong is worse than broken. Broken you fix. Slightly-off you just live with, quietly annoyed, until one day you snap.

Today I snapped.

First stop: the icon packs I already own, the ones sitting in iCloud that I keep telling myself justify their price. Surely someone drew a folder. No. Envelopes, clipboards, scrolls — a whole stationery shop, no folders. Who makes an icon pack without a folder? Rejected the lot.

So I made my own. Pasted in a folder icon I liked and let the machine do the tracing — I pointed, it drew, duotone SVG out the other end. Then it retinted the thing from its boring navy and grey into the app's palette: the red chocolate and the mango shade, colors I named months ago and still stand behind. This is the agentic thing working exactly the way I want it to. I didn't touch a path point. I just kept saying "warmer" and "no, the other layer" and watched it happen. Then the fun part: three variants — single folder, pair, stack — swapping them live with hot reload like trying on jackets. Change a line, watch the screen, feel it. This is the part of the work I'd do for free. The stack won immediately. A stack of folders says *cases* — plural, accumulating, work waiting inside. The single folder just said "folder." The pair said nothing at all.

Committed, pushed to the release branch. One icon, one commit. I genuinely thought that was the whole day.

It wasn't.

Because somewhere in the afternoon the folder stack got ambitious. The stats sheet has been sitting there half-empty for ages, and I've had this idea rattling around: show players how *fast* they're actually getting. Not a number. A curve. So I built a Performance section — a folder-tab panel, one tab per difficulty, the tabs generated straight off the difficulty enum so they can never drift out of sync, and behind each tab a density curve of your completion times per environment.

Actual density curves. Gaussian KDE over the times pulled from the XP ledger. And here's where the physicist in me — usually a liability, usually the guy making spreadsheets instead of shipping — finally got to earn his keep. The raw curves clipped ugly at zero, because of course they did, times can't be negative but the kernel doesn't know that. Reflected the mass at the zero boundary, extended the tail a couple of bandwidths past the 95th percentile so the curve fades out instead of getting guillotined, checked the area actually integrates to one. Twenty minutes of feeling like I still remember something from a past life. The curves came out *clean*. Three-pixel stroke, a fill at a third opacity, sitting on a white-washed inset tile. I kept opening the sheet just to look at them, the same way I kept triggering the folder icon this morning.

The colors fought back, though. Each environment needed its own representative color, and I fumbled it twice. First instinct: reuse palette colors I already had lying around — orient, watermelon, names that sound right and meant nothing here. Wrong. Second instinct: pick some tasteful pastels by hand. Also wrong — watered-down nothing on the cream background. Third try, the obvious one in hindsight: go to the source. Sampled the actual water sprite from the islands environment for its blue — cove water, I'm calling it, a proper #1cadf4 — sampled the pink trees from the grove for a blossom pink, #ff608a, and the cottage got the red chocolate it always secretly was. The environments already knew what color they were. I just had to ask them instead of inventing an answer. Then made it structural — a required `representativeColor` on every casebook, a proper registry, so no screen ever guesses an environment's color again. Two bad attempts and one decision I'll actually thank myself for.

Then the gating. You can't draw a density curve from two data points, so environments below a sample threshold show a progress bar instead — solve X more cases to unlock the chart. The threshold is a single constant, currently sitting at six, and I've already nudged it twice today. I'll nudge it again. Some numbers you don't calculate, you feel out. The nice part is that one constant drives everything — the gate, the bars, the copy — so nudging it is a one-character edit and the whole sheet reshuffles itself.

The gating meant empty-state copy in nine languages, which is where the machine nearly embarrassed me. The English said "unlock the plot." Plot, as in chart. Sonnet came back with plot, as in *storyline*, in every single one of the eight other languages. La trama. Die Handlung. Eight locales promising players they'd unlock the narrative if they just solved more sudokus. Caught it because Spanish is mine and "la trama" jumped off the screen like a typo in your own name. Hostia. Fixed all eight — gráfica, Diagramm, graphique, the words that actually mean chart. A reminder that the machine is brilliant right up until it's confidently, fluently wrong — and the human in the loop is not decorative.

The whole afternoon ran on the orchestrator pattern I've been settling into: Opus builds the hard thing — the KDE, the tab panel — Sonnet handles copy and progress bars, and Fable does the surgical one-value edits while I point at the threshold and say "lower." Conducting, not typing. It's fast in a way that still feels slightly illegal.

Committed, pushed, riding out with 1.13.0 alongside the morning's icon. One commit that says "Add Performance section to the stats sheet" and undersells the whole afternoon.

So the morning ledger said one icon and the physicist grumbled about the hours. By evening the same release branch has proper statistics on it — reflection-corrected kernel densities in a sudoku app, which is objectively ridiculous and I love it. Maybe five users will read those curves. But it's the same tax logic as the icon: I'll see them. And now when I do, they'll be right.

Venga. Next.
