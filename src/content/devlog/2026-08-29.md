Late afternoon commit. One file, one state machine, fifteen tests. The thing that decides when to ask someone "hey, do you like this?"

It sounds trivial. It isn't. Ask too early and you get a shrug. Ask right after a failed exam and you get a one-star review from someone who just found out they'd have failed the real thing. So: only after the first completed lesson or evaluation. Never inside an exam. Exam questions don't even enter the machine — they're not allowed in the room.

And the little trade I'm quietly proud of: the sentiment sheet comes first, and answering it unlocks three practice questions before the ads kick in. Not a bribe exactly. More like a handshake. You tell me how you feel, I give you a bit of breathing room. Veterans — anyone who's already been through the ad intro or already got the native review prompt — skip the whole thing. Don't nag the people who've already been asked.

Pure state machine. No I/O, no widgets, just transitions. This is the part of the codebase I actually like writing. It's the closest thing to physics in the whole app — inputs, states, deterministic outputs, done.

Then I did something I'm still not sure about. I ran the same review twice, with two different models, both cranked to high. Not because I distrusted the code. Because I was curious whether they'd disagree.

They mostly didn't. Which was either reassuring or disappointing, I can't decide. Between them they found: docs pointing at nothing, an untyped map I'd been too lazy to shape, the hydrate step silently swallowing unknown versions instead of complaining, the veteran path throwing away a "loved it" answer it should have kept, a sheet-presented event that didn't bother checking whether the sheet was actually due, and a sentiment that could be replayed. Six things. None fatal. All real. I fixed them all, tests still green, analyzer clean.

Here's the thing I keep turning over. I believe code quality is overrated. I believe if it works, ship it. And yet there I was at half six, running two reviewers on a file that was already passing its tests, patching edge cases that maybe two users a year would hit. Was that craftsmanship or was that sharpening the knife because sharpening is pleasant?

Honest answer: a bit of both. The hydrate one — swallowing unknown versions — that one actually matters. Someone updates the app, the persisted state has a shape from the future, and the machine just... pretends nothing happened. That's the kind of bug that shows up as "the review prompt never appears" three months from now with zero stack trace. Worth the twenty minutes. The rest was polish. Fine. Polish is allowed on a Saturday.

What's odd about having two machines review the work is that it's the closest I get to a code review anymore. There's no team. There's no one to tap on the shoulder. So I manufacture a panel of judges and let them argue about my map types. The ghost audience, but with linting.

Then the part I said "should be small" about. The ad manager.

The problem was simple to state: interstitials could fire before the user had technically unlocked them, which made the whole handshake a lie. You can't promise three ad-free questions if the ad code has never heard of the promise. So: hold every interstitial until the onboarding says go. Practice, quiz completion, exam — all of it zero during the intro. The counter keeps ticking underneath so nothing drifts, and the very first ad after unlock is a house ad. Ease them in. Don't go from "welcome, tell me how you feel" straight to a car insurance video.

Small in concept. Less small in practice, because the state machine needed a home. A service that persists it as JSON, and — this is the bit that bit me — mutations have to wait for the load. Ran the dual review again, same two models, and one of them caught exactly that: a race where a mutation could land before the persisted state came back, and the machine would happily overwrite reality with a blank slate. The other thing it found was veteran drift — the old prompt and the fourteen-day fallback both need to mark you a veteran *in-session*, right there, so the sheet can't sneak in after ads have already started. Both applied. Both the kind of thing I'd never have seen at seven in the evening on my own.

The second reviewer's main note was less a bug and more a shrug: the sheet isn't wired yet. Which is true. Right now the old prompt and the fallback still do the unlocking. The machine exists, the ads obey it, but the actual "hey, do you like this?" moment is still the legacy path. So today's commit is honest infrastructure — the gate is real, the thing that opens the gate is next.

Committed at five past seven. I've spent the day building a very careful mechanism for asking a question I haven't yet asked anyone.

Next: the sentiment sheet itself, and then kill the old prompt service for good. Two slices. Maybe one if I'm feeling brave tomorrow morning, before the evening version of me gets a vote.

Good day, small day. Two files that do exactly what they're supposed to. I'll take it.
