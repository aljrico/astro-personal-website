Late afternoon commit. One file, one state machine, fifteen tests. The thing that decides when to ask someone "hey, do you like this?"

It sounds trivial. It isn't. Ask too early and you get a shrug. Ask right after a failed exam and you get a one-star review from someone who just found out they'd have failed the real thing. So: only after the first completed lesson or evaluation. Never inside an exam. Exam questions don't even enter the machine — they're not allowed in the room.

And the little trade I'm quietly proud of: the sentiment sheet comes first, and answering it unlocks three practice questions before the ads kick in. Not a bribe exactly. More like a handshake. You tell me how you feel, I give you a bit of breathing room. Veterans — anyone who's already been through the ad intro or already got the native review prompt — skip the whole thing. Don't nag the people who've already been asked.

Pure state machine. No I/O, no widgets, just transitions. This is the part of the codebase I actually like writing. It's the closest thing to physics in the whole app — inputs, states, deterministic outputs, done.

Then I did something I'm still not sure about. I ran the same review twice, with two different models, both cranked to high. Not because I distrusted the code. Because I was curious whether they'd disagree.

They mostly didn't. Which was either reassuring or disappointing, I can't decide. Between them they found: docs pointing at nothing, an untyped map I'd been too lazy to shape, the hydrate step silently swallowing unknown versions instead of complaining, the veteran path throwing away a "loved it" answer it should have kept, a sheet-presented event that didn't bother checking whether the sheet was actually due, and a sentiment that could be replayed. Six things. None fatal. All real. I fixed them all, tests still green, analyzer clean.

Here's the thing I keep turning over. I believe code quality is overrated. I believe if it works, ship it. And yet there I was at half six, running two reviewers on a file that was already passing its tests, patching edge cases that maybe two users a year would hit. Was that craftsmanship or was that sharpening the knife because sharpening is pleasant?

Honest answer: a bit of both. The hydrate one — swallowing unknown versions — that one actually matters. Someone updates the app, the persisted state has a shape from the future, and the machine just... pretends nothing happened. That's the kind of bug that shows up as "the review prompt never appears" three months from now with zero stack trace. Worth the twenty minutes. The rest was polish. Fine. Polish is allowed on a Saturday.

What's odd about having two machines review the work is that it's the closest I get to a code review anymore. There's no team. There's no one to tap on the shoulder. So I manufacture a panel of judges and let them argue about my map types. The ghost audience, but with linting.

Then the ad manager. I'd written "should be small" in my head and, for once, it was. The problem was simple to state: interstitials could fire before the user had technically unlocked them, which made the whole "answer the sheet, get three free questions" handshake a lie. So now every interstitial holds — practice, quiz completion, exam, all of it — until onboarding says ads are open. The counter keeps ticking underneath so the rhythm is intact when they arrive. And the first one after the unlock is a house ad, not a real one. Ease them in. Don't go from silence straight to someone selling car insurance.

The persistence bit was where it got fiddly. State lives in JSON on device, mutations wait for the load to finish before touching anything, and the old fallback paths — the legacy prompt, the fourteen-day timer — now mark the user as a veteran in-session the moment they fire. Because otherwise you get the nightmare ordering: ads start, then three questions later the sheet pops up asking how you feel. About the ads, presumably.

Ran the dual review again. Habit now, apparently. One model caught a load race and a veteran flag that could drift out of sync — fixed both. The other pointed out, correctly, that the sheet isn't actually wired in yet. The machine exists, the gate exists, but the thing the gate is waiting for is still a promise. Right now the old prompt and the fourteen-day fallback are what unlock ads in practice. Which means for the moment I've built a very careful lock for a door nobody walks through.

Just past seven I'd told myself that was the day. Two commits, one shape, the sheet as tomorrow's slice.

Then I didn't stop.

Third commit, twenty past seven. The door has a handle now. You finish your first lesson or evaluation, you hit the win screen, you leave it — and the sheet slides up. Love it? Off you go to the native review. Grudges, or a dismiss? Sheet closes. Done. Nothing else. I'd been toying with routing the unhappy people somewhere — a Discord, a feedback form — and killed it. Someone who just told you they're not thrilled does not want a second ask. Let them go back to studying. That's the whole point of the app anyway.

And the satisfying part: I deleted things. The old review prompt service, the pending-prompt host that used to lurk under every screen waiting for its moment. Gone. Exams no longer arm anything. The fourteen-day fallback still exists but only fires after the sheet has had its turn, so the nightmare ordering from an hour ago is now structurally impossible rather than just unlikely. Deleting code you wrote three weeks ago is one of the few kinds of finishing that actually feels good. Less machine. Same behaviour, but the honest version of it.

Dual review, third time. One model found a hang-on-throw, a mismatch between the instance I was constructing and the one being injected, and an analytics event I'd forgotten to emit. Applied all three, no drama.

The other one flagged something bigger. Not a bug. A rule. Google's in-app review guidelines apparently say you're not supposed to ask the user an opinion question before showing them the rating card — no "do you like the app?" filter in front of the real thing. Which is, precisely, the handshake. The thing I've been quietly proud of since four o'clock.

I sat with that for a minute. Then kept it. The gate *is* the product — it's what stops the failed-exam one-star and it's what buys the three questions of peace. Other apps in this space do exactly this and live to tell about it. So it stays, with a note to myself that it's a known exposure, not an oversight. Physicist brain says: expected value of a slightly bent guideline versus a review score that reflects the app instead of someone's bad morning. Easy call. Gut agrees. I don't get that alignment often.

Still. There's a small itch there. The kind that turns into a rejected build in three months. I'll be watching.

So the full flow, as of tonight: first topic or evaluation complete, sheet, three practice questions of quiet, one house ad, then the existing ramp. Four hours ago that was a diagram. Now it's a thing a real person taps.

Morning-me wanted a state machine. Evening-me got the machine, the gate, the wiring, a deletion, and a policy question I've decided not to lose sleep over. Better than close enough.
