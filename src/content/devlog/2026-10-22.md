Started convinced the balance was close. Wasn't.

Pushed the perf patch (clamp helper) and round-2 tweaks. Ember at 5/8/13. Wave-mix genes for the evolved bot. The hybrid-seeded run was pulling 17+ waves where the old one barely hit 14. That felt like vindication.

Then I ran the full ladder and the edifice cracked.

The evolved bot was good at exactly one thing: Bloom + Lance in a specific ratio. The neutral bot went straight Ember and hit 20 waves. Even the hybrid start plateaued—CMA-ES stuck in a local optimum. Two days of compute and the answer was: I'd trained a bot that was excellent at one strategy and couldn't break out of it.

So I built a search tool. Beam search from scratch—what's the cheapest defense that survives wave 11? No evolution, no machine learning. Just: what does the search pick?

Needle + Ember.

Not Bloom. Not Frost.

I'd spent 18 hours tuning Bloom's reload and range. The search said it didn't matter.

Rounds 3, 4, 5 happened in a blur. Dial the knobs. Ember gets overtuned (bots hit 30 waves, sims take 3 minutes each). Bloom stays dead. By hour 20 I was tired and the search was right.

This is the analysis trap. I know the shape of it: the spreadsheets feel productive. The overnight runs feel like progress. But the answer was simpler and faster and I didn't see it because I was committed to the expensive approach.

Starting cash to 250. Ember 7/11/16. Bloom buffed on range (user's call—range identity over DPS parity). Bloom went from 3% of builds to 12%. Also discovered: 20% of dailies are unwinnable at 150 cash. Every bot dies by wave 4. That's not balance. That's broken.

There's a voice that says: you're not good at this. Someone smarter tries search first.

There's another that says: you didn't know that until you tried the other thing.

Both are true.

Shipped round 1 to TestFlight. Then I looked at the campaign page and stopped thinking about balance for a while.

It was three muddy cards with blurry circle lenses. 60% of the screen empty. User hated it. Fixed it: bright hand-picked pastels per region, bigger lens with soft white rim, light backdrop. The colours worked better with the original side-by-side layout, the one I'd almost thrown away. Sometimes the first instinct is right. Added a yellow Play tag to unlocked cards—flat #F8C66A, white bold text, bottom-right. Matches the Mini Motorways reference the user shared.

Swapped the fonts entirely. TeX Gyre Heros (bundled, Google Fonts, the GUST license already in the tree) everywhere Nunito was. Helvetica won't bundle on Android. The wordmark stays ARCO. Heros reads cleaner at display sizes, and once I committed to it, everything else followed—UI titles, campaign cards, the whole grid. White text on #F8C66A reads thin on WCAG contrast, but that's what Mini Motorways does, so we kept it.

Home page next. Killed the logo, drew a TeX Gyre Heros wordmark ("Mini TD.") and painted a city grid with a road and invaders walking patrol routes at game pace via a Ticker. The invaders were still the old four shapes, all angles, unloved. Spent the evening redesigning the whole roster.

Six sketches per critter, kept the ones that felt alive. By midnight I'd settled on something better. Soft single-eyed swimmers: mote=drop, swarm=long protozoan with a travelling wave, tanks=squat superellipse with twelve swimming tail lobes, colony=five pulsing mote-sized members. Stroke-driven outline, one centred eye. Tested a dozen other forms—bells, spirilla, flocking specks, beads—and burned them all. These felt right. Shipped them to TestFlight (build 14) and the roster suddenly felt worth building an entire world around.

The next iteration came fast. Motes became tadpoles, swarms became water fleas, tanks became mantas, colony became a rosette of six full-sized darts instead of five motes—each a real dart with full health, so the waveWeight math worked in sixths. Built 15 went out the next moment. Better. The creatures started to feel like they belonged in a place.

By 5pm we had a campaign structure. Four regions, all placeholder levels still, but they existed as cards with hand-picked postcard crops showing what made each region different. But the user rejected the Mini Motorways-style compact grid—too small, too samey. We reworked it into a journey. A white road with regions as stations down it, each one a lens onto a level that captures that region's character. Softwood (canyon, narrow meanders), Apricot Bay (coast, bays, headlands), Moss Valley (now the opening, easiest by definition), Rust Mesa (big mesa with a hidden river tunneling under it). Three simplify passes on the campaign UI and it started to read cohesive.

The journey layout took shape: regions alternating left and right down the road, each ringed by 25 level stops (region shade = cleared, gold = 3 stars). Road dashed in Play yellow, filling as you cleared levels, so the next region unlocked at 10 clears. Winding paths inside each region too—no straight grids. Softwood gentle meanders, Apricot Bay long sweeps, Moss Valley tight switchbacks. Per-region character baked in at the generator level.

Softwood got 25 authored levels first. Then Apricot Bay with a completely different feel: a lake that shaped itself per level via headlands (juts of cliff 3-4 columns from a side), small rocks strewn across the land. User loved them. Moss Valley next with narrow canyon walls (neighbour-weighted growth, smooth Chaikin contours) that breathed in and out as you descended. The rivers stayed just barely wider than a tower. Rust Mesa last: a full-width rock band with rivers that could tunnel underneath, giving the region its own difficulty without needing a handicap.

Each region got its own palette. Moss: earthy greens. Apricot: warm sand and terracotta. Softwood: cool blues and greys. Rust: rust and dust tones. The campaign card lenses painted live, locked regions veiled behind white, cleared ones bright, all five in a row so you see the journey ahead.

Added a Field Guide while momentum carried. Two tabs: invaders with Latin names and Lovecraftian notes (a joke, but it landed), and towers. Each tower card runs on a real Softwood board zoomed so you see it work. Redesigned tower selection: drag-to-build with a floating ghost, reach preview in tower colour, all the juice. Red bursts for leaks, yellow for bounties, counters that tick up one-by-one.

By 10pm I'd wired up a tower unlocking system. Stars from campaign levels unlock new towers: Needle and Bloom free, Frost at 25 stars, Ember at 50, Lance at 100. Renamed Lance to Lens—the charging effect via fragment shader drains light around the focal point while 9 lilac motes accelerate in from 1.5 cells. After a win's stars land on the end card, a dark toast drops from the top: "FROST UNLOCKED" or the next tower dimmed with "29 / 50 ★" and a bar filling. That juice matters. The user seeing that counter climb is everything.

Then I noticed the greedy bot only builds Needles. All 30+ of them. Every level. Every region. The unlocking system was perfect except it was meaningless.

Back to balance. But this time I didn't spiral. The search tool had cracked the first 11 waves. Now I needed regional difficulty curves. Each terrain is different. Moss Valley canyon walls narrow the options. Apricot Bay's headlands split the river. Rust Mesa's tunnel trades space for vulnerability. The waves had to respect that.

Rewrote waves as floor formulas of difficulty x and wave w. x = 0.25 per region + 0.01 per level minus 1. Gating: wave 1 motes and swarms only, shells on even waves, darts on odd waves past 3, colonies rare. The lever was mote HP growth: 4·x·(w−1). A fractional multiplier quietly gave darts extra hits. Fixing that retuned everything.

Moss Valley opened the campaign. Cliffs now rise mid-level, alternating with river growth—a dashed footprint a wave ahead, build-blocked, then a 1.2-second slide up. Softwood stayed gentle. Apricot Bay added headlands and rocks. Rust Mesa with its tunnels went last. The search said Needle + Ember still. Fine. That's the answer. Let it be that.

Built a ladder tool—parallel sims on 8 cores, moved map gen to an Isolate so boards build in 20ms. Then faster: grids as quarter-cell with ray-cast fallback, Bloom SpatialHash with dense Int32List buckets. 1.8x faster on AOT. The slow path was always the generator, never the sim (the Opus vs Astra debate was settled by profiling, not philosophy).

Rounds 4 and 5 collapsed into each other. Tower costs kept shifting. Frost went from "doesn't exist" to "freezes everything but dies to anything fast" to something that stacks its chill effect per tower placed. Ember went 5/8/13 then 7/11/16 then 10/15/20. By round 5 it was 7/11/16 again—the user was right the first time. Bloom stayed at 250 cost, 3/5/8 damage with range 3/4/4.5. That range buff moved it from 3% of builds to 12%. It mattered.

The bots changed too. The evolved CMA-ES bot got 56 genes. Wave-mix genes (dart/crowd/tank) to break out of local optima. A casual bot that picks towers at random. Hybrid-seeded runs where the evolution starts from a hand-tuned strategy instead of zero. Every round the search was there: "what's the actual cheapest path through wave 11?" It was always Needle + Ember.

Built builds 14, 15, 16, all the way to 31. Campaign postcards, region lenses, invader redesigns, tower showcase in the Field Guide. Each build shipped to TestFlight, each one a little more real than the last. By the end of it I was shipping builds faster than I could count them.

There's a thing you learn: you can't solve balance before humans touch it. You ship. They find breaks you didn't simulate. Some things you only know once someone plays it for real.

I want to ship and see what breaks. But first the journey has to feel alive. And today—today it does.
